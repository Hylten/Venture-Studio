The market funds the wrong companies, and the studios know it. Per Euclid Ventures' The Vertical Report 2026, vertical AI captured 53 percent of venture deals while absorbing only 30 percent of venture capital. Horizontal AI absorbed the rest of the money. The gap between deal share and capital share is the studio's operating field, because it means the most capital-efficient companies in the market are being built on the least capital per employee.

The distinction that matters is velocity versus efficiency. Capital velocity measures how much money a company raises per year since founding. Capital efficiency measures how much it raises per employee. Per the same Euclid dataset, Life Sciences leads velocity at 4.8 million USD raised per year, while Education trails at 1.6 million USD. Education raises roughly 342,000 USD per employee, the leanest profile in the report. The verticals where studios concentrate, legal, accounting, construction, sit closer to Education than to Life Sciences.

## The Seed Signal

The momentum is at the earliest stage. Euclid's stage-level analysis shows vertical share in the 1 to 5 million USD tier, the pre-seed and seed domain, rising from 53 percent in Q1 to 60 percent in Q4, an eight-point gain that the report describes as one of the most significant forward-looking signals in the dataset. Three of five new venture-backed companies now build for a specific industry. The post-ChatGPT generation is not building general-purpose tools. It is building AI for healthcare, legal, construction and manufacturing.

The aggregate capital picture reinforces the point. Per Fastio's 2026 market analysis, AI startups captured 242 billion USD in Q1 2026 alone, about 80 percent of all global venture capital for the quarter, roughly 2.7 trillion SEK. The money is abundant at the top of the market. The question for a studio is not whether capital exists. It is what the capital costs in ownership, and that is where the efficiency gap becomes an edge.

## The Studio's Cost Position

A studio builds the product, the data layer and the customer base from a shared platform. The first company absorbs the platform cost. The second and third inherit it. That sequence produces a raised-per-employee ratio that independent founders cannot match in the early rounds, because the independent founder raises for the same capabilities the studio already owns.

The valuation side compounds the effect. Euclid's data on median valuation step-ups shows Aerospace and Defense leading at 2.15x, Manufacturing at 2.08x and Real Estate at 1.96x. Financial Services, despite attracting the most capital, shows a more modest 1.80x step-up, because larger round sizes dilute the per-round markup. The pattern rewards companies that raise little and step up hard. That is the studio's geometry: low capital in, high step-up out.

## The Unit Economics in SEK

The efficiency advantage translates directly into unit economics. As an illustrative planning assumption for a studio-built vertical AI company in the Nordics, consider a customer acquisition cost of 18,000 SEK and a monthly recurring revenue of 9,000 SEK per seat with a 24-month average contract. The LTV lands near 216,000 SEK before churn, a ratio above 10 against CAC. A horizontal competitor raising at 3x the studio's burn faces the same LTV with a capital structure that demands a different exit.

The edge case belongs in the analysis. The studio model concentrates risk in the platform: if the shared data layer serves none of the verticals, the efficiency becomes a liability, because the platform cost was paid upfront. The discipline that protects the model is portfolio-level capital allocation, each new vertical funded against a demonstrated customer pull, not a conviction slide deck.

## What the Exit Buyer Sees

The exit market prices efficiency. A vertical AI company with 15 million SEK in ARR, a 342,000 USD-equivalent raised-per-employee profile and a documented data moat presents a cleaner multiple than a peer that raised 80 million SEK to reach the same revenue. The buyer is buying the ratio as much as the revenue, because the ratio predicts the post-acquisition burn.

Per the benchmark data, vertical step-ups at the early tier reward exactly this profile. The studio that builds three verticals from one platform and sells them separately captures the step-up three times. The independent founder captures it once, diluted by the capital required to get there.

## The Constraint

The constraint on the studio model is not capital. It is selection. The vertical must be fragmented enough that the platform wins, specific enough that the workflow is teachable, and large enough that the ARR compounds. Studios that violate the selection criteria build efficient companies in small markets, and efficiency does not compensate for a ceiling. The model works when the vertical, the workflow and the buyer are all three defined before the first line of code.

## Summary

Vertical AI takes 53 percent of deals and 30 percent of capital because the capital-efficient companies are being built on the least money per employee. Studios exploit that gap through shared platforms, low raised-per-employee ratios and step-ups that reward discipline. The seed tier is where the momentum lives, with vertical share rising to 60 percent of 1 to 5 million USD deals. The studio's job is to select the vertical before it builds, because efficiency without a market ceiling is a well-run trap.
