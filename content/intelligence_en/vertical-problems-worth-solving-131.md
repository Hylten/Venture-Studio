## Problems Worth Solving — A Filter Model for Identifying 100M+ ARR Verticals

## Introduction: Why Vertical Problems Require a Different Lens

In an era where general platforms grow faster than their real use cases, there is a structural need to define what is actually a problem worth solving. Venture building in the AI era tends to gravitate toward attractive technologies rather than historically entrenched system gaps. The result is often an oversupply of generic solutions that never achieve the necessary dominance within a specific domain.

Verticals capable of reaching over 100M ARR are never random. They are characterized by system-heavy processes, structurally recurring information flows, regulated or capital-intensive infrastructure, and a clear asymmetry between those who own data and those who exploit it. They are also often marked by low technological penetration and a tradition of locally optimized workflows that make modernization require both domain understanding and significant integration capacity.

Building in these verticals does not primarily mean replacing processes, but reconstructing the underlying information systems that govern them. It requires an institutional perspective, where the focus is on how data moves through a value flow, which actors influence it, and what technical constraints have historically defined what behavior was possible. In practice, this is a question of system economics rather than traditional product development.

This article establishes a filter model for identifying exactly these verticals: markets where a new player can build significant, recurring revenue streams by controlling system logic rather than offering yet another tool. The goal is to distill a set of criteria that makes it possible to assess not only whether a problem is interesting, but whether it is structurally and technically possible to build a category owner that achieves market dominance.

## Market Geometries: How a 100M ARR Vertical Differs from the Rest

The central mistake in many venture strategies is the assumption that all verticals fundamentally function the same way. They do not. Verticals that can carry over 100M ARR have a recurring geometry: a combination of structural complexity, regulated or costly information processing, and a fragmented actor base lacking the ability to build modern infrastructure themselves.

Three observations recur:

1. Verticals that reach this scale are not primarily large, but deep. They have enormous chain length of activities and dependencies, creating opportunities to become the central system to which everything else adapts.

2. Revenue distribution within these verticals is often characterized by intermediaries, consultants, or manual process operators representing convertible spend. When such an ecosystem is digitized, a significant portion of historical cost mass can be shifted to software fees.

3. Data flows in these verticals are often chronically incomplete. This means an actor who can create a structured data pipeline quickly becomes the standard, since no one else has a complete representation of the process.

This provides a fundamental guideline: if a vertical does not contain a data arbitrage opportunity, it is rarely a 100M+ opportunity. In these markets, value is not in automating, but in establishing a correct, unbroken information chain.

## Why AI Accelerates Vertical Convergence

Before 2023, vertical software was often limited by a lack of adaptive logic. Systems were built statically around processes expected to change slowly. AI infrastructure, especially in the form of model composition, agent flows, and domain-specific embeddings, has made it possible to build systems that can absorb variation rather than collapse under it.

For the first time, it becomes possible to create systems that:

- map fragmented processes without requiring standardized data protocols.
- automatically adjust rules, templates, and workflows based on historical deviations.
- connect heterogeneous data sources without long integration cycles.
- recreate human decision steps in a process where these were previously bottlenecks.

This adaptive ability says something fundamental: vertical systems no longer need to be defined by legacy processes, but can define how these processes should function going forward. This means categories previously too fragmented to support centralized systems are now accessible.

But it also means something else: competitiveness is no longer defined by features, but by how well an actor can model a flow’s real-time state. Whoever owns the data model owns the value flow, and thus the market.

## Systems Thinking as a Core Principle: Flows, Nodes, and Constraints

To identify which verticals can become category owners, you must shift from market analysis to system modeling. The question should not be how large the market is, but how the information flow is structured.

Three parameters are central:

1. Where are the critical nodes in the process, where decisions or validations occur?
2. Where does data leak, and what consequences does this have for the actors?
3. What regulatory, economic, or technical constraints have historically prevented modernization?

If a vertical is characterized by many nodes, high complexity, and low data quality, a natural opportunity for dominance arises. A new system can centralize the nodes, improve data quality, and reduce the number of actor dependencies. It is this centralization that makes it possible to build recurring revenue at scale.

Here, system configuration becomes more important than the product. Products that are modular, AI-adapted, and flow-centered can adapt to the vertical’s natural logic. Products that are form-based or workflow-heavy risk becoming another layer in an already complex system.

This is why vertical SaaS platforms with the potential to reach 100M ARR almost always have an embedded data platform, not as an add-on but as the core. The data platform is what enables AI automation, real-time validation, and insight production. The products above are just interfaces to it.

## Filter 1: Information Complexity as a Structural State

One of the most consistent patterns in large verticals is that information complexity is not a temporary problem but a structural state. It does not arise because actors lack digital systems, but because the vertical’s logic requires linking many parallel decision processes.

A vertical is structurally complex when:

- data points depend on multiple actors’ input before becoming valid.
- decision logic is not linear but consists of iterations and revisions.
- deviations in one step require recoding of several subsequent steps.
- data must be coordinated between private, public, and semi-regulated entities.

When these characteristics are permanent, legacy systems are practically unable to represent the process correctly. They become documentation tools instead, creating a vacuum where a new system can take over process control itself.

AI reinforces this opportunity by being able to:

- interpret unstructured data and integrate it into the flow.
- detect deviations without predefined rules.
- suggest sequence changes based on historical outcomes.
- automate validation of incoming data in real time.

The result is that information complexity is no longer an obstacle to vertical dominance. It becomes instead a filter: the higher the complexity, the greater the chance a new actor can become system owner.

## Filter 2: Data Monopoly Through Flow Positioning

The most valuable vertical systems do not take over the market through functionality, but by positioning themselves exactly where data passes. It is the difference between offering value and controlling the value flow.

There are three types of positions that can provide data monopoly:

1. Upstream capture: The system becomes the natural point where all new data first originates.
2. Midstream normalization: The system becomes the central hub where data is validated and standardized.
3. Downstream verification: The system becomes the instance that certifies data or decision logic before it moves forward.

A vertical that contains the opportunity to take any of these positions will almost always become a potential 100M+ category. To assess this, you must understand:

- which actors produce original data
- where in the flow data changes
- where data gains regulatory or economic significance
- which decision nodes are critical for the entire chain

When an actor gains control over midstream or downstream flows, something interesting happens: all other actors must adapt, because their own processes become dependent on the data being correct. This creates a form of vertical lock-in that is difficult to compete with, even if alternative tools were cheaper or faster.

## Filter 3: Systemic Waste That Can Be Internalized by a Platform

Large verticals are often characterized by extensive systemic waste: time, capital, personnel, and data lost through inefficient processes. When this waste is built into the structure rather than a consequence of poor systems, the opportunity arises to build a platform that internalizes the value.

Examples of systemic waste:

- double registration between authorities, operators, and intermediaries
- consultant work for validation or reconstruction of data
- manual controls of rules, compliance, or agreements
- fragmented customer journeys where information is repeated
- capital tied up due to long decision processes

When the waste is structural, it means:

- there is a clear, quantifiable cost mass
- the cost is recurring and stable
- the actors accept the waste as a normal part of the vertical

This again means a platform that can absorb the process can take over corresponding cost mass through recurring fees. The economic logic here is very predictable: if the waste exceeds a certain threshold, there is room for a 100M ARR system without the market needing to grow.

The critical question becomes: can AI enhance this internalization by making the process self-correcting? In many verticals, the answer is now yes, opening a new class of systems previously too complex to build.

## Filter 4: Fragmented Operator Base but Centralized Regulator or Capital Structure

Another recurring characteristic of large verticals is the asymmetry between those who drive the process and those who control the frameworks for it. Typically, we see:

- many small or medium-sized operators
- one or few regulating instances
- a capital structure that is centralized or standardized
- processes requiring all actors to follow the same rules or protocols

This asymmetry makes it possible to create a vertical standard, since the operator base lacks the resources to oppose a new system logic as long as the regulator or capital owner implicitly supports it.

This means the most effective path to dominance often goes through:

- building the system as an infrastructure tool for regulators or capital owners
- creating standardized data models that operators must follow
- offering integrations that reduce operators’ internal system costs

The important thing is to understand that in asymmetric verticals, adoption is not created bottom-up, but through structural support. This is often more stable and more predictable than traditional SaaS distribution.

## Filter 5: The Ability to Reconstruct the Vertical’s Logic Through AI Infrastructure

With modern AI infrastructure, you cannot only automate existing processes, but model them anew. This is a qualitative difference that changes the selection logic for which verticals are attractive.

A vertical is particularly suited for AI reconstruction when:

- there are large amounts of unstructured data currently unused
- decisions are based on history, case similarity, or pattern recognition
- rules change frequently and require rapid implementation
- there is variation between different operators but they follow the same overall logic

In such verticals, you can create systems that:

- automatically generate new rules and workflows
- build representation models for each actor or process
- perform quality controls previously requiring experts
- predict deviations before they occur, based on data patterns

The central point is that AI makes it possible to establish a form of vertical intelligence: a system that not only contains the data, but understands the process.

When an actor achieves this, the system quickly becomes irreplaceable, since competing solutions lack the historical data model needed to recreate the same insights.

## Summary: The Filters Are Not Theoretical, But Operational Tools

What characterizes successful vertical SaaS systems in an AI-first era is that they are built with an understanding of the vertical’s fundamental system economics, rather than its visible problems. To identify which verticals can carry a 100M+ ARR actor therefore requires a model that focuses on flows, data, and structural complexity.

The five filters described here are not a checklist in the traditional sense, but a method for understanding which problems are structurally inevitable and therefore worth solving. A problem is only worth solving when it can not only be automated, but when automation changes the system’s foundational logic.

In the next part, the model is developed through concrete frameworks, operational examples, and structures for how to identify, validate, and sequence these verticals in practice, as well as how AI infrastructure affects prioritization in each phase.

## Filter 3: Dominant Cost Centers That Cannot Be Ignored

One of the most reliable ways to identify a vertical with the potential to reach over 100 million in recurring annual revenue is to map the cost centers that the business absolutely must manage to survive. These costs are not just large but frequent and embedded in business logic. If you can solve a central cost driver with software and create a clear before and after, there is often exponential willingness to pay.

These are costs that do not leave the business alone. Payroll, materials, logistics, insurance, compliance, and operations are classic examples. A dominant cost center acts as a guarantee that the customer will continue paying even in a downturn, because the alternative is risking core operations. This means solutions that reduce, automate, or reallocate these costs can expand deeply into a vertical and create both great value and strong retention.

A common misconception is that verticals with many cost centers are always good targets. In reality, it is better to have few but heavy cost centers. The clearer the pain point and the more directly tied to the operational engine, the better. The vertical should have an inherent systemic burden requiring constant decisions, administration, and management. There, a software provider can take the place of an operational hub.

The important thing is not to be fooled by the visible cost. If a cost is large but rarely changes, it is difficult to build software around. If a cost is small but occurs in high volume and is complex to manage, it can create significant value. It is the relationship between volume, frequency, and criticality that determines how valuable a solution can be.

A clear example is labor. Most industries with shift-based staffing suffer from the same problem: scheduling, absences, skill requirements, local rules, and productivity tracking. This is not just a large cost, but a cost that is constantly changing and operationally central. This is the type of structural requirement that can carry an entire vertical software platform.

The ability to strike directly at one of the vertical’s main organs is one of the strongest indicators that it can reach large scale. It is not about improving something nice to have, but about affecting something that must function every day.

## Filter 4: Real Bottlenecks in Production or Decision Making

Once you have identified the largest cost centers, the next filter is to find the bottlenecks in how value is produced. All verticals have some form of flow, even if it looks completely different in a hospital compared to a transport company or a construction project. In this flow, there is one or two steps that determine the pace of the entire operation. These steps function as the vertical’s pulsating heart.

A bottleneck is not just an inefficient moment but the moment that limits everything else. If that bottleneck can be enlarged, automated, or controlled more intelligently, the entire vertical’s output increases. This makes solutions near the bottleneck very valuable and difficult to compete with once they are in place.

The important thing is that bottlenecks often do not appear in traditional cost accounting. They are operational phenomena, not economic ones. This means verticals can sometimes look uninteresting on paper but be extremely attractive if you map how work is actually performed.

A common characteristic of attractive verticals is that the bottleneck is person-dependent. Several of the world’s most successful vertical software companies started by digitizing a process previously residing in the head of a certain type of expert. When dependence on an individual decreases, production and stability are unlocked. It is difficult to overestimate how much value this creates in industries with skill shortages.

Another indicator is that the bottleneck often creates domino effects. If it fails or is delayed, it impacts several other steps. This means customers not only want to solve the problem but are forced to solve it. A solution that stabilizes or accelerates the bottleneck quickly becomes a system other parts of the organization must integrate with.

Bottlenecks create natural stickiness mechanisms. When a provider controls or visualizes the most critical step in the customer’s value creation process, it becomes difficult to replace the solution. The customer is not bound by contract, but by the logical structure of their own operations. This is the most sustainable form of retention.

## Filter 5: Non-Negotiable Compliance and Risk Management

Society’s increasing complexity means many verticals face growing pressure from regulation, certification, and risk control. This is often a sensitive issue. Few businesses want to spend more time on compliance, but the cost of failure is so great that the need becomes absolute. This type of non-negotiable risk creates attractive environments for software.

The critical aspect of this filter is that regulation is not only extensive but dynamic. Verticals affected by constantly changing requirements become dependent on systems that automatically keep them updated. This gives the provider inherent expansion power because the product not only solves today’s problems but tomorrow’s. The customer buys security and stability, not a specific function.

Another characteristic is that compliance is distributed across many units or individuals. If a rule requires hundreds or thousands of employees to follow the right routine or document the right thing, software is often the only realistic way to ensure compliance. This creates structural lock-in. The customer cannot stop using the system without losing their risk management.

Risk-driven verticals also tend to generate large amounts of data. Every event, measurement, control point, or deviation becomes a data point driving new features. This means the product often organically broadens. It may start as a documentation system but grows into an analytics tool, a planning module, and operational support. It is difficult for competitors to attack such a product without matching both the data volume and the customers’ history.

Compliance is also one of the few areas where willingness to pay is almost always stable. When regulations tighten, demand for solutions that create order and visibility increases. This makes verticals with heavy compliance attractive even in periods of low growth, since customers cannot decommission systems.

## Filter 6: Fragmented Ecosystems with Many Dependencies

A vertical does not need to be large to be valuable. It needs to be complex. One of the clearest indicators of an attractive vertical is that it consists of many different actors who are all dependent on each other and need synchronized information for work to flow. This type of fragmented ecosystem is a perfect environment for software that can function as a common coordination surface.

Fragmentation can take different forms. Sometimes it is geographic, where several small actors work in parallel without centralization. Sometimes it is functional, where each step in the chain is handled by different companies with their own systems. Sometimes it is temporal, where processes occur in stages with many handovers. The common factor is that no one has full oversight and the friction in information transfer is costly.

Verticals with this structure respond extremely well to products that standardize communication, formats, reporting, and status. It is not always necessary to build the most advanced product. It is enough to be the system everyone must use for the process to function. Whoever succeeds in placing themselves as the hub quickly gains network effects.

Another advantage is that fragmented verticals often have low digitalization to begin with. This means competition is limited and the first solution that creates real coordination becomes difficult to replace. Once workflows are standardized around a solution, collective lock-in arises where even small actors must follow the system’s structure to access the work.

The real leverage lies in the fact that such a vertical often generates a lot of repetitive communication. Every handoff, reconciliation, approval, and status update is a data point that can be automated or enhanced by the system. This leads to the product growing deeper into the ecosystem and eventually becoming an infrastructure tool.

Fragmentation also creates scalability. Once a provider has cracked the code in one geographic area or subsegment, the model can be replicated in others. The vertical does not need to be global from the start, but it must have the same fundamental structure across multiple markets. This means expansion can occur with low adaptation and high margin.
