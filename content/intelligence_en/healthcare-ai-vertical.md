## Introduction: A Shift from General Models to Domain-Specific Systems

Healthcare’s digital infrastructure is at a stage where general AI models no longer suffice to solve the most critical problems. What is now emerging is vertical AI systems that are strictly adapted to clinical workflows, regulatory requirements, and industry-specific data models. This shift is not a cosmetic improvement. It is a paradigm shift in how healthcare’s decision systems are built, implemented, and governed.

As AI moves closer to patient-facing decisions, pharmacological recommendations, documentation automation, and clinical diagnostic models, both the value and risk profile increase. Thus, the development of vertical AI in healthcare is a matter of creating a new type of infrastructure, rather than merely packaging model capacity into yet another product.

This is an environment where system-building replaces app-building, where data quality replaces model size as the primary competitive advantage, and where ethical considerations are not external to the business model but integrated into the technical architecture.

## Why Vertical AI Is Especially Critical in Healthcare

General models can generate text, reason across broad knowledge domains, and perform general prediction tasks. But in healthcare, the knowledge domains are not only specialized; they are also heavily regulated and systematically structured. This means AI systems must:

- understand medical semantics and coding systems
- interpret heterogeneous data types such as lab results, imaging diagnostics, sensor streams, and clinical notes
- comply with regulatory frameworks that vary between regions, countries, and healthcare organizations
- document decision pathways in a way that is auditable after the fact

All of this requires vertical models that integrate domain expertise directly into their structure. The question is not whether general models can be fine-tuned for health data, but how to build systems where the entire architecture reflects healthcare’s logic. Vertical AI systems in the industry therefore do not function as a layer on top of existing workflows, but as structurally embedded components in the operational infrastructure of care.

## The Data Foundation: When Quality Trumps Quantity

There is a common misconception that more data automatically leads to better models. In healthcare, the opposite is true. High data quality in a limited, semantically well-defined domain outperforms large but unstandardized datasets.

The technical challenges are not just about collecting data, but harmonizing it. Medical records can vary dramatically between different care providers, despite following similar standards. Terminologies differ between clinics, even within the same region. Timestamps are inconsistent. Data is missing but reported as complete. Contextual information such as patient status, pregnancy, comorbidities, or drug interactions lacks structure and requires semantic interpretation.

This means that work on vertical AI must begin with systematic data modeling, not model training. The central question is how to build an interoperable data layer that allows continuous validation, traceability, and explainability. In practice, data modeling therefore becomes a form of ethical architecture: incorrect structure leads to incorrect recommendations, and incorrect recommendations lead to real risks.

## Technical Architectures That Meet Regulatory Requirements

Regulatory requirements in healthcare are not obstacles; they are structural design parameters. Therefore, the architecture of a vertical AI system is at least as important as the model’s predictive capacity. Systems need to:

- include built-in version control for model parameters
- maintain audit logs for every prediction
- enable replay of data sources and decisions for retrospective review
- support role-based access control
- accommodate regional data dialects and local adaptations
- separate training data from active patient data

These are requirements that traditional SaaS infrastructure has not handled by default. This creates a new type of technical design philosophy: AI that is regulatorily compatible from the ground up, rather than retrofitted. For organizations scaling from Series C and above, this means that it is no longer sufficient to build product features. They must build institutional systems capable of bearing long-term clinical and legal scrutiny.

## The Difference Between Automation and Delegation in Clinical Environments

A central misconception is that AI should automate large parts of healthcare’s manual work. It is more accurate to view vertical AI as a system for delegation, not automation. Automation is binary: either a task is performed by a human or by a system. Delegation is relational: a task is performed by a system, but under human oversight and with the possibility of reconsideration.

In healthcare, delegation is the only sustainable model. AI systems can generate clinical summaries, suggest prioritization orders, or flag deviations. But ultimately, it is healthcare professionals who must exercise their professional responsibility, and AI systems must be built to support that control.

This requires a user interface that is not only functional but cognitively adapted to clinicians’ decision-making processes. If an AI system for clinical note analysis, for example, produces a recommendation, it must be clear which data points it is based on. Clinicians must be able to see the system’s reasoning without having to interpret model architecture or code. This means that explainability in healthcare AI is not an afterthought. It is the foundation of interaction design.

## Embedded Ethics: From Policy to System Logic

Ethics in AI is often discussed as principles, but in vertical AI, ethics must be implemented as code. This means the system needs to be able to:

- restrict models from making recommendations outside their domain
- document when uncertainty is too high to provide a clinically relevant answer
- alert the user when data is missing or insufficient
- ensure risk assessments are conservative when the consequences of incorrect predictions are significant

It is not enough for AI systems to be powerful. They must be responsible from their operational mechanisms. This requires a system-building perspective where robustness is optimized before raw performance. In practice, this means that a model that is slightly less capable but more predictable is often preferable to a model with higher precision but lower stability.

## Ecosystem Architecture: Collaboration Across Healthcare’s Technical Layers

A vertical AI system cannot be isolated from the rest of healthcare’s infrastructure. It must integrate with:

- EHR systems and local IT installations
- national health record registries
- biobanks and specialized data sources
- medical technology platforms
- patient portals and real-time monitoring

Collaboration is extensive, and integrations are often the biggest bottleneck for scaling. In an environment with many proprietary formats and regional variations, an ecosystem strategy based on interoperability as a competitive advantage is therefore required. Systems that succeed in establishing standardized data flows between actors become hubs in a larger network of decision support and clinical tools.

This raises a strategic question: should a company in vertical healthcare AI optimize to be a standalone product, or should it build an infrastructure platform that other actors can build upon? In practice, the latter is often necessary to achieve long-term production at scale.

## Risk Management as an Operational Capability

The healthcare sector is unique in that every AI system, regardless of purpose, carries an inherent risk profile. Risk management must therefore be an operational competence, not a reactive discipline. This means:

- continuous real-time monitoring of model performance
- automated detection of drift
- regular recalibration cycles
- local validation in every clinical environment where the system is implemented
- clear risk classes for different types of recommendations

When AI is used to prioritize patients, interpret symptoms, or suggest treatments, the organization must be able to answer three central questions: What is the model doing? Why is it doing it? And under what conditions should it not be used?

This requires development organizations to build internal processes for regulatory documentation, clinical validation, and technical quality control even before the system reaches the market. For Series C+ companies, this means that operational quality in risk management is as important as the product’s innovation height.

## The Structural Limitations of General Language Models

General models have impressed with their flexibility, but when used in healthcare, their limitations become clear. They lack:

- internal representation of medical causality
- robust handling of temporal dependencies
- structured understanding of event chains in patient cases
- ability to interpret complex medical imaging data without additional specialized models
- consistent semantic precision in diagnostic reasoning

This means that even if general models can be used as an interface layer for clinicians, they cannot stand for the central medical understanding. That must lie in specialized, vertical models trained on high-quality clinical data.

When the strengths of these two layers are combined, you get both user-friendliness and clinical robustness. But it requires a system architecture that bridges two different types of model capability. This is something many organizations underestimate in the early development phase, but it becomes critical when systems are scaled broadly in clinical environments.

## The Path Forward: Toward a Unified Model Architecture for Clinical Decisions

The long-term development in healthcare AI is moving toward model architectures where foundation models form the language and interaction layer, while vertical models provide clinical logic and decision support. For this to work in practice, a new generation of middleware is required that handles:

- semantic translation between clinical data structures and model input
- aggregation of heterogeneous data sources
- contextualization of real-time data in relation to historical patient data
- strict rule sets for what models are allowed and not allowed to do

This is the beginning of a more unified system for AI in care, where the model is no longer the core of the product, but a component in a larger operational ecosystem. Systems must be created with a clear understanding that clinical AI is not a software product, but a form of technical stewardship. A stewardship that includes clinical understanding, technical robustness, regulatory compliance, and ethical sustainability.

When these parts are integrated, a new type of AI system emerges: not a tool that assists care, but an operational infrastructure that care relies on. It is in this shift that vertical AI shows its true potential, but also where the greatest risks arise. Therefore, the next central question is: how do you build governance and decision frameworks that scale with the technology’s capabilities, without...

## When Healthcare’s Vertical AI Becomes a Black Box

One of the most concrete problems with vertical AI systems in healthcare is that they often become difficult to audit. Models are trained on extensive medical specialty datasets and implemented in clinical workflows where no one outside the development team fully understands how recommendations arise. This is not just a technical issue but also a democratic and ethical problem.

When decisions affecting people’s health are based on algorithms that are opaque, a gap arises between responsibility and consequence. Who is responsible if the AI system makes a mistake? The physician who trusted the system’s recommendation? The hospital that purchased the technology? The developer who built the model? Or a combination of all? In practice, responsibility often falls on healthcare professionals, despite their lack of conditions to understand the model’s inner logic.

This is why a growing debate among both European and Swedish researchers revolves around the need for transparency requirements that go beyond those currently discussed in the EU AI Act. Some argue that full openness is unrealistic because models are commercial assets and complexity is too high for even AI experts to describe every step. Others argue that healthcare is an area where openness must be primary, even if it comes at the expense of companies’ protected information. This conflict will intensify as more clinical decisions are automated.

## When Data Becomes Both Resource and Risk

Vertical AI companies working specifically with health data have a business model built on access to vast amounts of sensitive information. Swedish and European hospitals hold valuable datasets that are extremely attractive to companies wanting to build competitive models. At the same time, this data requires the highest possible integrity management.

Historically, Swedish healthcare has had strong traditions of secure data management and strict regulations. But the pressure to digitize care has created new openings for partnerships between public institutions and private AI vendors. Every time data leaves the traditional infrastructure, the risk of misuse, leakage, or unclear ownership increases.

A common scenario is that companies offer hospitals advanced AI analysis in exchange for using the data to train their models. From the care perspective, this sounds like a good deal, but once the data has left control, it can be used to develop systems that are commercialized globally in the future without Swedish patients or care units benefiting in return. This creates an asymmetry where the public sector delivers the raw material while the private side takes almost all the value.

Researchers and authorities have long pointed out that this type of data exchange must be regulated more strictly. Pseudonymization is not enough because modern techniques for re-identification are highly powerful. The issue is not just about privacy but also about national self-determination. Who should control the models that are trained on Swedish and European health data in the future? Should they be owned by global corporations or by societal actors representing citizens’ interests?

## The Dual Nature of Clinical Decision Support

When AI systems are used as decision support in clinical environments, quality and efficiency often improve. Triage can happen faster. Diagnoses can become more accurate. Patient flows can be optimized. At the same time, the line between support and control becomes increasingly blurred.

A system that presents a recommendation may seem harmless, but in practice, several studies have shown that healthcare professionals tend to follow algorithmic suggestions even when unsure of their correctness. This phenomenon, known as automation bias, is well-documented in aviation, nuclear power, and finance. In healthcare, where consequences are deeply personal, it can have even greater significance.

As systems improve, it becomes even harder for a physician to justify deviating from the recommendation. If the AI model shows higher accuracy than human expertise, it also undermines trust in traditional professional competence. This risks changing the relationship between healthcare professionals and patients. The idea of the responsible physician is gradually replaced by the idea that the best decision is the one the model suggests.

This creates a situation where healthcare professionals need new competencies. They must understand how models work, when they should be questioned, and how to assess their limitations. Without this competence, there is a high risk that AI systems will steer decisions more than intended. This is why several Swedish regions are now discussing how to train staff to collaborate with advanced AI tools without losing critical capabilities.

## When Efficiency Meets Ethical Boundaries

One of the main arguments for vertical AI in healthcare is that systems can solve resource crises. Automated triage and automated diagnostics are presented as solutions to staff shortages and overload. It is true that AI can alleviate certain bottlenecks, but the effect depends on how systems are implemented.

Many experts warn that the efficiency argument can create a dangerous rationality where the purpose slowly shifts from improved care to pure cost optimization. When decision-makers see results in the form of saved working hours and lower expenses, it can become tempting to let AI replace rather than complement staff. This in turn changes the relationship between patient and care system.

Patients are not production units, and care is not an industrial process. When AI is used to standardize assessments, care risks losing its human flexibility. A system optimized for average outcomes may miss individual needs. People with unusual symptoms or complex medical conditions risk falling between the algorithm’s predefined categories.

The ethical dilemma is that a highly optimized AI can be statistically correct while creating injustices for individuals. To avoid this, transparency is required in how optimization goals are defined. What does the model prioritize? Fastest flow? Cost efficiency? Medical accuracy? Or equity in care? These goals often conflict and must be clarified before the technology is deployed.

## The Regulatory Landscape’s Challenges

The EU AI Act has created a basic structure for how risk classification and oversight should occur. But many experts argue that the regulation is too general to meet the specific challenges of vertical systems built on health data. Health is a domain where risks are more complex than the functionality of algorithms. Data flows are more sensitive. Clinical environments are more dependent on responsibility allocation. The consequences of errors are greater.

Sweden stands at a crossroads. Either it relies on the EU framework, or it develops national additions that account for Swedish needs and public structures. Several regions are already requesting more guidance on the requirements for AI models integrated into care’s IT systems. They want to know which standards apply to data quality, logging, evaluation, risk analysis, and continuous monitoring.

There is also a lack of clear processes for how models should be decommissioned when they no longer meet requirements. An AI model is not static. It ages, loses relevance, and is exposed to new patterns it was not trained for. If the model is not updated regularly, it risks providing increasingly poor results, but every update is itself a new risk. This leads to a paradox where renewal is necessary but also problematic.

## The Growing Need for Independent Quality Controls

Something that is becoming increasingly clear is that care cannot rely on vendors’ own quality assurances. A clinical environment requires independent review of both the model’s technology and its effects in practice. This is about ensuring that AI tools work as promised, not just during testing but after implementation.

Several researchers propose that Sweden should establish national test environments where models can be quality-assured before use in care. Such test environments would need access to realistic datasets and clinical simulation environments. Review would need to include both statistical performance and assessment of risks for bias, overfitting, inequality, and systematic errors.

Another need is continuous monitoring. An AI model may perform well on launch day but degrade over time. Care’s systems must therefore be able to detect when models start producing anomalous results or when data patterns in the population change. This type of monitoring requires both technical infrastructure and staff competence. In practice, this means that every AI model entails a long-term maintenance responsibility rather than a one-time installation.

## Concluding Reflections Before the Next Part

Vertical AI in healthcare offers great utility but simultaneously creates a range of structural, ethical, and clinical challenges. Care needs the tools but must understand their limitations. Regulation exists but is not yet sufficiently adapted to the specific risks in medical environments. Data is a resource but also a vulnerability. And efficiency must not come at the expense of humanity and justice.
