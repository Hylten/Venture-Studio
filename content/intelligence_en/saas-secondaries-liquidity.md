## Introduction: A Market in Structural Transformation

Over the past decade, European and Nordic SaaS companies have undergone a structural shift. Previously dependent on traditional capital flows, many have now reached a level of maturity where their financial systems, data infrastructure, and market positions begin to resemble those of public companies. The expansion of AI infrastructure, data flows in cloud environments, and subscription-based revenue models have increased the need for capital allocation in a more sophisticated way than early venture finance historically needed to support.

In this environment, secondaries are emerging as an operational tool rather than an exception. Secondaries previously primarily gave early investors an opportunity to realize returns. Now, they are used as a system component in how Series C and later SaaS companies optimize their capital structure ahead of a potential IPO. It is no longer about freeing up value for individual shareholders, but about building stability in an increasingly complex ecosystem of institutional investors, data-driven expansion teams, and AI-first product strategies.

It is against this backdrop that secondaries emerge as a key mechanism. They connect liquidity, governance, incentive structures, and market signaling in a phase where the company needs to demonstrate institutional maturity rather than entrepreneurial flexibility.

## Why Secondaries Have Become a Systemic Need in Mature SaaS Companies

In the SaaS sector, the transformation of capital structures has accelerated. This is due to three overlapping forces:

1. A growing institutional ownership landscape where pension funds, infrastructure investors, and large international funds have different expectations regarding liquidity and risk management than traditional VC actors.
2. Higher production complexity in the companies’ technical systems, especially after the AI transformation of 2022-2025, which has created more capital-intensive expansion phases in go-to-market, data processing, and model training.

3. A market that requires longer private lifecycles before IPO, often 10 to 12 years, which means that incentive structures for founders, operational teams, and early employees need to be adjusted to remain functional.

Secondaries fulfill a function that traditional new capital cannot solve. New capital dilutes ownership, changes investor composition, and often requires new expansion plans. Secondaries, on the other hand, do not directly add capital to the balance sheet, but they stabilize the human and institutional infrastructure around the company. For SaaS companies where data infrastructure, long-term customer contracts, and model development are central value drivers, stability is a strategic asset in itself.

## The Institutional Logic Behind Secondaries

When a SaaS company passes €30 to €50 million in ARR, it begins to attract investors who see the company as a long-term infrastructure object. These investors want predictability in the ownership base. A constant flow of small shareholders seeking liquidity creates uncertainty and can hinder larger capital allocations.

Secondaries solve this by:

1. Consolidating the ownership base.  
2. Creating a softer transition to the governance structures institutional investors require.  
3. Preparing the company for the transparency and internal control requirements that come with an IPO.

Institutional investors want to know that the company is not facing internal tensions. They want to see that incentive programs are balanced. They want to be sure that management is not facing personal financial constraints that could affect strategic decisions.

Secondaries thus become part of the institutional due diligence process. They signal that the company has control over its internal structures and is willing to create a predictable capital environment that can withstand scrutiny.

## How Secondaries Stabilize the Leadership Team Ahead of IPO

A common problem in the late stage is that key individuals hold illiquid shares while their private risk exposure is extreme. AI-driven SaaS companies tend to grow rapidly but also invest heavily in data processing, model training, and automation. This often requires high-risk management decisions, even if the company is successful.

When the IPO window may be several years away, an imbalance is created: management and early teams theoretically own large values, but cannot realize them. This is organizationally unsustainable.

Secondaries solve this by giving the team controlled liquidity. It is not about cashing out, but about removing personal financial risks so that key individuals can continue to make long-term decisions.

From a systemic perspective, this is about:

1. Relieving personal financial risks.  
2. Anchoring the team in a multi-year expansion strategy.  
3. Strengthening the internal ability to maintain pace in product development.

When risk levels are neutralized, the strategic quality of decisions increases. This is central at a time when data processing, model optimization, and security architecture require investments that must be realized over longer horizons.

## Secondaries as a Capital Allocation Tool

It is common to view secondaries as a pure liquidity mechanism, but in mature SaaS companies, they are also an indirect capital allocation tool.

When the ownership base is consolidated and investors with shorter investment horizons leave, space is freed up for long-term institutional actors. These investors are often willing to support the company in:

1. Data center expansion.  
2. Acquisitions of complementary AI technologies.  
3. Expansion of security infrastructure.

Thus, secondaries become part of the capital strategy despite no capital flowing in directly. They improve the company’s structural conditions for future capital raising.

Secondaries therefore also affect the cost of capital indirectly. A company with stable ownership and well-calibrated incentive structures can negotiate capital on better terms. This is relevant in a market where SaaS multiples vary significantly depending on the quality of the company’s internal systems.

## The Technical Dimension: Why AI SaaS Strengthens the Need for Secondaries

AI-first SaaS companies build products that are not static. They are systems that are continuously trained, often in real time, and require a continuous flow of data, compute resources, and model updates. This creates three capital-intensive structures:

1. Increasing costs for model training.  
2. Need for dedicated infrastructure, sometimes on-premise.  
3. Competency-dependent teams where retention is critical.

In such a system, organizational stability becomes part of product quality. A team that is financially stressed or divided can unconsciously hold back important decisions regarding security architecture, delivery models, and experimental features.

Secondaries are therefore not a soft value. They are a component of how companies build technical resilience. As the company approaches an IPO, stability becomes a regulatory and industrial prerequisite.

## Market Data: The Increasing Share of Secondaries in Late Rounds

Statistics from US and European tech markets show a marked increase in secondaries in rounds above Series C. In some segments, secondaries account for over 30 percent of the total capital volume in a round.

This reflects a market where:

1. The IPO window is more unpredictable than before.  
2. Private equity and crossover funds have become active owners in SaaS.  
3. Companies actively optimize their ownership base ahead of public markets.

The Nordic market follows this pattern but lags slightly in volume. This means that secondaries in the Nordics are still an underutilized strategic tool. Several companies reach Series C with a fragmented ownership base that could have been stabilized much earlier with a controlled secondary process.

## Governance as Part of the Liquidity Strategy

When a company plans an IPO, the focus shifts from vision and growth to governance and control. The market expects a clear ownership structure, predictable decision paths, and a systematic risk program.

A well-designed secondary helps to:

1. Reduce the number of small owners and private blocking risks.  
2. Prepare the cap table for institutional requirements.  
3. Reduce friction in the preparation of the IPO prospectus.

This is not a cosmetic measure. Governance is directly linked to valuation in the public market. Companies with complicated ownership structures are almost always traded at a discount compared to those with a clean cap table.

Secondaries are therefore a tool for governance hygiene. It is an investment in the company’s long-term ability to be valued correctly in the public market.

## How Secondaries Affect the IPO Preparation Phase

A SaaS company preparing for an IPO needs to show the market that it is ready to function as a public system. This involves requirements for:

1. IFRS accounting and clear revenue classifications.  
2. Predictable churn patterns.  
3. Stable EBITDA profile.  
4. Clear governance and accountability.

Secondaries belong in the same category. They contribute to creating a liquidity situation where key individuals do not have private incentives that conflict with the company’s public strategy.

In practice, this means that secondaries should be seen as part of the IPO playbook. They are not an isolated phenomenon but a structural component. As the company moves toward a public market, it needs an ownership base that can act long-term and is not forced into unexpected extraordinary transactions.

## Risks of Avoiding Secondaries for Too Long

There are SaaS companies that wait too long. They often hope that the IPO window will open quickly and thus make secondaries unnecessary. But when the market delays IPOs, three risks arise:

1. Increased internal friction when teams and early investors have different time horizons.  
2. Weakened competitiveness in recruiting senior talent, who often require predictable liquidity.  
3. Complicated capital raising because investors see that the company is deferring structural problems.

In a market characterized by AI-driven quality pressure, where every decision affects both technology and capital structure, secondaries become a necessary valve. When they are absent, systemic tensions build up that sooner or later impact expansion speed and market value.

## When the Company Should Initiate Its First Major Secondary

Timing is a structural issue. In mature SaaS companies, the optimal window often arises between:

ARR €20 to €40 million  
Series B to early Series C  
Growth rate between 30 and 80 percent  

At these levels, investors begin to see the company as a potential public object, but the company still has the flexibility to adjust its ownership structure without disrupting IPO plans.

The spring before a potential pre-IPO round is often too late. The processes then become reactive rather than systemic.

## How Secondaries Change the Dynamics Between Founders, Employees, and Investors

As a SaaS company approaches an IPO, the relationship between its key individuals changes. Everyone sees the same goal within reach, but incentives can still differ. This is why secondaries play a particularly important role ahead of an IPO.

In an early stage of the company’s growth, everything is about surviving the next quarter. When the company, however, has reached the scale-up phase and plans to go public, the horizon is longer and the demands are higher. At the same time, the pressure on those who have carried the company through its most risky years grows.

A well-designed secondary gives these individuals a completely new type of security. When they are no longer dependent on a future exit for their private finances, focus often becomes sharper. It becomes clearer what is required for the business to stand strong in a public environment. The incentives between founders, management, and investors come closer together.

The opposite is also true. If individuals in key roles feel that their financial future stands or falls with a specific date on the calendar, risks are created. Short-term thinking, stress-related decisions, and conflict lines can emerge. This is one of the reasons why growth-stage investors increasingly encourage a controlled secondary. It makes the company better equipped for the final stretch to the public market.

## Liquidity as a Tool to Secure and Motivate Talent

One of the most underestimated effects of secondaries is how they affect talent acquisition. SaaS companies in late growth phases are typically dependent on employees with unusually specific skills. This applies to both product development, GTM structures, security, compliance, and financial control.

In these fields, competition is not only with other startups but with global tech companies offering salaries and conditions that few private companies can match. Incentives in the form of stock options therefore become a central part of the compensation model. The problem, however, is that options in a private company often mean a long and illiquid journey.

One possible solution is to offer liquidity via a secondary. This creates two effects:
1. Employees with significant option positions get the opportunity to realize part of their value. This reduces the pressure to leave and take a high-paying job elsewhere.
2. The company can expand its option program more aggressively when it knows there is a future mechanism for liquidity.

For companies in late SaaS phases, especially those targeting an IPO within 12 to 24 months, this can be critical. The internal narrative for employees becomes stronger. They see that hard work is not just something that may pay off in the future, but something that is partially rewarded even before the actual listing.

## Balancing Control and Flexibility in a Pre-IPO Structure

Ahead of an IPO, the structure of the cap table is one of the most scrutinized factors. Banks, legal advisors, and potential investors want to see clarity in the ownership base. Here, an unplanned secondary can create problems, while a planned and structured secondary can make the cap table more investable.

The challenge is balance. On the one hand, the company wants to allow some liquidity. On the other hand, it must avoid losing strategic control. This is especially true in companies where founders and early investors have key roles even after an IPO.

Three factors are central to this balance:

1. **Buyer Character**
A secondary ahead of an IPO should always be limited to buyers who do not disrupt the company’s long-term structure. Typical buyers are existing investors, growth funds, or specialized secondary players. Never allow opportunistic actors without strategic relevance.

2. **Volume Sold**
There is a clear rule of thumb in pre-IPO phases: liquidity should strengthen the company’s resilience, not change the power balance. If a founder sells 5 to 10 percent of their holdings, incentives are barely affected. If a founder sells 40 percent, the dynamics change radically.

3. **Timeframe to Expected IPO**
The closer to an expected listing, the more limited secondaries become. Therefore, an optimal window often arises between 18 and 36 months before listing. After this window, valuations and regulatory requirements tend to lock the process.

When these factors are managed correctly, secondaries can facilitate an IPO rather than complicate it. The cap table becomes more homogeneous. Individuals in key positions gain more stable incentives. And advisors see a clear, controlled structure suitable for public trading.

## How Bankers’ and Advisors’ Views on Secondaries Have Changed

Ten years ago, secondaries ahead of an IPO were viewed with some suspicion. Bankers often considered it a signal of a lack of confidence in management. If management wanted to sell now, why would the market buy later?

Today, the perspective has shifted. In fast-growing tech sectors, especially SaaS, it is now considered unsustainable for key individuals to go all the way to IPO without some form of liquidity. Investors know that stable teams, long-term focus, and clear incentives are needed. Present this picture early in the dialogue with advisors and you will notice that most are open to secondaries, as long as they are proportional and transparent.

This does not mean that bankers accept anything. However, they realize that secondaries can be a tool to achieve what the market demands: focus, resilience, and an organization ready for the demands of the public market.

## Common Structures for Secondaries Ahead of an IPO

When SaaS companies approach an IPO, three main structures are used. Which is best depends on the company’s situation and goals.

1. **Investor-Led Secondary**
An existing investor buys shares from employees or founders. This is the most straightforward model and often the one that causes the least disruption in the cap table. For investors, it can also be a way to increase their exposure ahead of the listing.

2. **Organized Tender Offer**
A structured process where a limited number of buyers are offered to buy shares from a defined group of sellers. This provides structured liquidity and suits companies with many employees holding options or shares.

3. **Buyback-Like Structure**
In some cases, the company itself can buy back shares within the framework of applicable regulations. This is less common in pre-IPO SaaS companies but can be used to clean up the cap table or manage early investors who want to exit.

The most common structures in the Nordics and Europe are investor-led and tender offers. They provide high predictability and low risks. Above all, they create a clear narrative both internally and externally: the company is moving to the next phase while offering reasonable incentives to those who have made the journey possible.

## The Psychology Behind Liquidity Ahead of an IPO

The psychological dimension is often the most overlooked. As a SaaS company grows rapidly, life changes for everyone in the organization. Pressure increases. Demands increase. And at the same time, this is not immediately reflected in anyone’s bank account, because the value is tied up in shares that cannot be sold.

A controlled secondary changes this dynamic. It creates a shift in focus, where future value creation can take greater precedence over short-term risks. This is why many investors see secondaries as a way to increase the likelihood that the IPO actually happens. Psychological stability is a business factor.

It is not about individuals becoming rich before the company goes public. Rather, it is about giving the team the security to continue making the right kind of decisions. It is when people do not have to worry about their personal finances that they can fully focus on product, customers, and growth.

## Conclusion: Liquidity Is Not a Reward, It’s a Strategic Tool

When looking at secondaries in SaaS companies ahead of an IPO, it is easy to believe that it is about rewarding founders or early employees. But in practice, it is something entirely different. It is a strategic measure that strengthens the company, improves its resilience, and increases the likelihood that an IPO will be successful.

Liquidity ahead of an IPO is part of building a company that can meet the demands of the public market. It is a way to align the organization’s incentives and ensure that the right people stay on board during the most critical 12 to 24 months.

Done correctly, a secondary does not just create security for the individual but also stability for the company. And it is this stability that often determines whether an IPO becomes a milestone or a missed opportunity.
