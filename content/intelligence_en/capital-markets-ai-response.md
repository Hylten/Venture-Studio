Capital Markets’ Response to AI: Why Funds Are Restructuring Portfolios in 2026

## Introduction: A New System Layer Takes Shape

The capital markets are in one of their most significant transformation phases since the breakthrough of cloud architecture. In 2026, the question is no longer whether artificial intelligence adds value, but what type of AI can be deeply integrated into financial flows, data architecture, and industrial processes. For venture funds and institutional investors, the challenge is to recalibrate their investment models toward a technological shift that moves from consumer-facing applications to system-critical infrastructure.

The movement is palpable. Funds that historically focused on SaaS, platform technology, and horizontal enterprise applications are now increasing the share of capital allocated to raw data, model compilation, inference layers, and sector-specific AI operating systems. This is not a trend-driven repositioning but a structural realignment, where the future returns of portfolios correlate directly with how well they reflect the next layer of digital production.

AI has become an infrastructure issue affecting everything from compute economics and data access to organizational capacity. In the capital markets, it is therefore seen as necessary to understand AI not as a product but as a transformation matrix that reshapes how companies are designed, scaled, and measured.

Industry assumptions about scalability, margin structure, and product cycles are now being reassessed in real time. And amid this, funds face the need to restructure their portfolios to meet a market where competitive advantages are increasingly built into the model, the data layer, and the execution of automated decision flows.

## Capital Markets in 2026: An Accelerating Split Between AI Applications and AI Infrastructure

From 2023 to 2025, the investment climate was dominated by consumer-facing generative AI applications. This was an extension of the SaaS era’s logic, where the application was seen as the primary value driver. But in 2026, a different shift has emerged: investors now clearly distinguish between the application layer and the infrastructure level.

Funds conducting systemic risk assessments identify that the application layer is reaching maturity faster than expected. Barriers to differentiation are increasing, margin pressure is intensifying, and the model is becoming homogenized. This creates an investment landscape where value is increasingly tied to layers that enable uniqueness—namely, data architecture, proprietary model pipelines, and operational workflows.

AI infrastructure is now divided into three primary domains:

1. **Compute and model compilation**
2. **Data management, preprocessing, and controlled data access environments**
3. **Operationalization of AI through agent frameworks and automated pipelines**

For the capital markets, this means that the ability to analyze technical debt, data sharing efficiency, and model dependencies becomes central parts of the due diligence process. Funds are adapting their teams, recruiting technical partners, and building internal models to assess latency, token cost, dataset layering, and model optimization—factors that previously lay outside traditional financial analysis.

It is precisely this split between application and infrastructure that is driving the wave of portfolio restructuring in funds. The application layer continues to grow, but the risk profile is changing. Infrastructure, on the other hand, is perceived as more robust because it addresses bottlenecks in the global AI ecosystem.

## Systemic Stress: The Compute Economy and Its Effect on Capital Allocation

What is accelerating the transformation is the new compute economy. The transition from traditional cloud services to specialized inference capacity means that cost structures are changing fundamentally. AI models require not just training capacity, but continuous inference that must be optimized to be economically sustainable in production environments.

Investors are forced to analyze companies based on three technical cost drivers:

1. **Model configuration and training cost**
2. **Inference latency and token cost**
3. **Data needs, including pipeline automation and verification**

Companies that build their models on public cloud infrastructure without optimization end up in a cost spiral incompatible with venture logic. This is why many funds in 2026 are actively reassessing their existing holdings and prioritizing companies that control critical parts of their inference and data stack.

Controlling compute economics is now seen as a necessity for achieving scalability. This does not mean that all companies must build their own models, but they must demonstrate technical ownership over their production environment. This becomes a central parameter for the capital markets in assessing long-term value.

## Data Layer and Proprietary Data: Capital Markets’ New Raw Material

The most strategic shift driving portfolio realignment is the insight that the data layer is now the primary competitive advantage in the AI economy. Even if the model can be swapped or optimized, the dataset is often irreplaceable. This has changed how investors analyze companies in early and late stages.

Funds now review:

- **Data validity and uniqueness**
- **Data structure and degree of normalization**
- **Ability to use data for future models and use cases**
- **Rights, licensing status, and risk exposure**

This is a significant change. Previously, data was often evaluated as an underlying asset rather than as a primary vector for value creation. In 2026, data has become the infrastructure enabling high capacity utilization in AI systems.

Of particular interest is funds’ increased focus on vertical data. Horizontal datasets have reached maturity, while sector-specific datasets in areas such as logistics, financial automation, industrial production, and energy offer higher value because they are harder to duplicate and require domain expertise to operationalize.

Portfolios that historically lacked data-heavy companies are now being restructured to increase the proportion of companies with controlled data models and the ability to generate ongoing data value.

## Venture Funds’ Transformation: From Product Evaluation to Architecture Assessment

Venture funds with traditional SaaS focus are now operating from a completely different thesis. They no longer analyze product functionality or market potential alone, but place increasing emphasis on system architecture. This includes how the company manages model governance, ML-ops, pipeline automation, and how its data environment is constructed.

This creates a new type of due diligence where the focus is on:

- **How the model integrates into the customer’s production systems**
- **Whether data logistics and model monitoring are automated**
- **How the model improves over time and at what pace**
- **The degree of technical debt embedded in the model and data layer**
- **Dependencies on external model providers**

Funds are also focusing on how companies design their internal structure. AI companies with strong production structures often have cross-functional teams between data engineering, model development, and product design. Those lacking this tend to be built in a traditional SaaS manner, meaning the model is added rather than integrated.

All of this affects valuations and investment decisions. Funds that previously invested broadly in SaaS are beginning to divest or restructure parts of their portfolios in favor of system-heavy, AI-embedded companies. The risk profile is different, but also much more aligned with where the market is heading.

## The Industrialization of AI: A New Phase in the Technology Cycle

In 2026, the most important driver behind portfolio restructuring is that AI has entered an industrialization phase. This means the technology is no longer seen as experimental or an add-on to existing systems, but as a core component of companies’ operational infrastructure.

Industrialization entails three fundamental changes:

1. **AI becomes part of transaction flows, not just analysis flows**
2. **Production teams integrate AI into core systems instead of peripheral functions**
3. **Model updates become a continuous process rather than a one-off effort**

This creates an entirely new investment logic. Historical success factors in SaaS, such as user-friendliness or quick time-to-value, are being replaced by other criteria:

- **Ability to execute continuous inference at industrial scale**
- **Control over model improvement and data recycling**
- **Automated pipelines that minimize manual intervention**

The industrial maturity of AI is also creating demand for interoperable systems. Investments are moving toward platforms that enable multiple models and data sources to run in parallel within the same production flow. This shift is driving the entire capital market toward companies that can become hubs in customers’ data infrastructure rather than functioning as isolated applications.

Funds are restructuring their portfolios because positions that are not architecturally integrated risk rapidly losing value in competition with companies building toward the new industrial AI economy.

## Macro Effects: Regulation, Standardization, and Consolidation

Regulatory changes also play a crucial role. The EU AI Act and corresponding U.S. frameworks have created a situation where companies must prove traceability, risk control, and secure model management. This strengthens companies that build controlled infrastructure and weakens actors with weak data governance.

The capital markets interpret this as investment risks now being more technical and regulatory rather than market-driven. This leads to:

- **Model traceability becoming a requirement in due diligence**
- **Companies with closed data chains being preferred**
- **Companies relying on unclear licensing models being valued lower**

This change also creates a consolidation logic. Funds see that smaller AI application companies will need to merge with platforms offering better compliance, more robust model management, and controlled inference environments.

All these macro factors are driving portfolio realignments because funds are seeking positions where regulatory stability and technical control are built into the business model.

## Conclusion: Capital Markets’ System Logic Is Changing

Funds are restructuring their portfolios because AI infrastructure has become a new production layer on a global scale. Value is no longer primarily created in applications but in the systems that enable their scaling, adaptation, and robustness. This changes the capital markets’ fundamental assumptions and creates a situation where new types of companies—with deep control over data, models, and pipelines—are perceived as the market leaders of the future.

This realignment is not a temporary positioning but a permanent structural change. And as AI becomes more integrated into industrial systems, capital markets’ preferences will continue to shift toward companies that can build and own the next generation of AI operating systems, from data layers to model compilation and full-scale inference operations.

It is in this context that funds’ portfolio realignments must be understood. They are not reactive. They are a consequence of a fundamentally new technical architecture that is shaping the global production logic. As we move into the second half of 2026, the question is how quickly these changes spread to adjacent sectors and which doors in the value chain open for the next generation of AI companies to...

## Changed Risk Models and New Volatility Signals

As investment houses entered 2026, one thing became clear. The traditional risk models that had underpinned capital allocation for decades were no longer able to predict market movements in an environment where AI was driving both information flows and trading decisions. The long-held insight that the market reflects human behavior began to unravel as machine-based decision-making accounted for an increasing share of volume.

In several major Swedish funds, a marked weakening of models based on historical patterns was noted during the first half of the year. Volatility spikes came more often without warning, and intraday movements became more erratic. The explanations were many, but one of the most recurring was the effect of AI tools acting on news, analyses, and data points before humans even had a chance to register them.

This drove a wave of model updates. Risk teams began integrating real-time indicators from AI-driven market surveillance systems, and several firms developed hybrid frameworks where human analysts make qualitative assessments that are weighed alongside machine signals. One fund manager internally described the market as no longer driven by emotions but by algorithms’ interpretations of emotions. This created a need to reassess everything from portfolio weights to how defensive assets are defined.

## The New Hunt for Quality in Data

As more and more investors began leaning on AI in their decision-making processes, a new competitive advantage emerged. It was no longer the models themselves that separated winners from losers, but the quality of the datasets feeding those models. Institutional investors began investing heavily in their own data platforms and customized data sources such as satellite imagery, patent filings, alternative logistics chain data, and industry-specific real-time streams.

A Swedish pension fund noted that data costs increased by over 40% in two years, while the internal data science department was significantly expanded. Managers reported a sense that traditional fundamental analysts no longer stood a chance if they did not have the same data coverage as actors using AI to synthesize hundreds of thousands of signals. This also changed how analysis departments were organized. In several cases, data engineers, AI specialists, and sector analysts were integrated into the same teams.

The result was a clear movement toward data monopolies within niches. Those who control better data get a more accurate model, which in turn creates better returns and more capital to reinvest in new data sources. This spiral has become one of the clearest structural shifts in capital markets during 2026.

## Rethinking Traditional Fundamental Analysis

Fundamental analysis is not dead, but its role is changing. At the start of 2026, it became clear that AI could often analyze financial reports faster and find correlations that humans miss. This initially caused concern among analysts. But during the year, a new insight emerged. The human analyst is needed more than ever, but the work methods must be reshaped.

AI can predict earnings revisions and margin changes better than previous tools, but this does not mean the machine understands companies’ strategic positions. Analysts are therefore forced to focus more on qualitative assessments and discussions with company management. The valuation of soft factors has become a competitive advantage. This includes everything from organizational culture to leadership’s ability to integrate new technology.

Funds that found the right combination of machine breadth and human depth saw clear performance in the first quarter. This gave a new direction where the analyst’s role shifted toward formulating hypotheses and questions that AI systems then tested. The classic Excel model has been joined by iterative simulations and scenario engines working in the background.

## Portfolio Movements Toward AI-Driven Value Chains

One of the most notable shifts in 2026 was how capital management changed toward companies that directly or indirectly benefit from AI. It was no longer about just investing in tech companies. A broader analytical model emerged where funds assessed how well each company in the portfolio was positioned to use or integrate AI into its own operations.

This created three categories of winners.

**First category:** Companies whose products are built on AI. These were already growing rapidly, but after 2024’s regulatory clarifications on generative AI, investor confidence increased further. Swedish and Nordic companies in analysis software, industrial automation, and cybersecurity saw accelerating capital inflows.

**Second category:** Companies that streamlined internal processes using AI. Here, managers saw major opportunities in logistics, medtech, and banking. Companies that could demonstrate increased margins through AI-optimized processes were quickly rewarded with higher valuations.

**Third category:** Companies facing structural risk due to AI. These became the losers. Many funds began scaling down holdings in labor-intensive companies with limited digital investments. This particularly affected sectors where AI could replace human roles on a large scale, such as simple services, customer service, and parts of retail. This rotation even affected index weights in some European markets.

## The Rise of AI-Driven ETFs and Fund Platforms

At the same time as capital was being restructured within traditional funds, a new market layer emerged. ETFs controlled by autonomous AI models increased significantly in popularity. In Sweden, two new platforms were launched in 2026 where AI selected portfolio weights in real time based on market flows, sentiment data, and macroeconomic sensors.

What was unique was that these funds did not follow classic quarterly rebalancing. Instead, weighting changed continuously, creating an entirely new type of market dynamic. Critics argued this could amplify short-term volatility. Defenders highlighted that the model delivered higher risk-adjusted returns.

Among institutional investors, a clear curiosity was evident. Many viewed these ETFs as tools for tactical allocation rather than core holdings. There was also an insight that AI-driven portfolios create new correlation patterns that can be exploited in arbitrage-like strategies.

## Regulatory Shifts Changing the Playing Field

The European AI law phased in at the end of 2025 began having concrete effects in 2026. For capital managers, this meant increased requirements for transparency in how AI models are used for investment decisions. This led several major Swedish players to build internal frameworks for model risk and audit traceability.

Regulators particularly emphasized explainability. An AI model taking positions in, for example, credit markets needed to justify its decisions in a way that was comprehensible even to non-technical authorities. This became a challenge because several of the most advanced models were built on deep networks that are not trivial to explain.

Funds that succeeded in navigating this new environment reinforced their competitive advantages. They could continue using advanced models without risking regulatory sanctions. Those slow to adapt were forced to scale back their AI components or switch to more conservative approaches.

Regulation also affected data collection. Data sources previously considered harmless were now classified as sensitive. This forced investors to review how data is stored, shared, and documented. It increased entry barriers for smaller players and gave larger institutions an unexpected advantage.

## Consequences for Swedish Institutional Investors

Sweden has long been known for its large pension funds and systematic investment philosophies. In 2026, the country also became an experimental workshop for how large institutions integrate AI on a broad front. The AP funds led the way with extensive pilot projects. These covered everything from bond portfolios with AI-based risk forecasts to stock selection where machines identified inefficient pricing.

The most interesting change was in decision-making processes. Instead of traditional investment committees meeting once a week, continuous digital flows were introduced where AI analyzed market signals in real time and managers made decisions when threshold values were passed. Humans retained the final say, but the machine set the agenda.

This created a faster and more responsive management environment. At the same time, internal competence development requirements increased. Several funds introduced training programs in AI ethics, model understanding, and data governance. For the first time in a long time, recruitment profiles changed. Funds began seeking specialists who could interpret the interaction between market behavior and AI logic rather than classic Excel analysts.

## A New Market Logic Taking Shape

When viewed collectively, it is clear that 2026 does not just represent a technological shift but a structural shift in the fundamentals of capital allocation. The market is moving toward an environment where human and machine perspectives interact in ways previously impossible.

AI has become a catalyst for restructuring. It drives faster information dissemination, new data requirements, changed portfolio logic, and volatility signals different from those the market is used to. For fund managers, this means a more complex but also more opportunity-rich reality.

What remains to be understood is how far this development can go. Will AI eventually dominate all major investment decisions, or will human intuition always retain a special status? 2026 marks the beginning of this new balance point, not the end. It is a time when the market is still forming, where rules are not fully established, and where competitive advantages shift faster than ever.

This means that the funds best able to adapt are not necessarily those with the most resources, but those that understand the interplay between technology, data, and human strategy. In this transformative phase, flexibility becomes the true guiding star. And it is precisely this flexibility that will define the winners of the capital markets as we move toward the end of the decade.
