Vertical AI consolidates around one asset: the workflow. Foundational models are commodity inputs.

Every studio in a vertical can call the same frontier model at the same price. The differentiation sits in the data the workflow generates, the integration depth that captures it, and the compounding loop that improves the product with every transaction.

The data moat is the moat. Model performance converges across competitors within a quarter. Workflow data compounds across years, because every customer run produces new labeled signal that no competitor can buy.

## The Commodity Layer

The model layer is a commodity with a shrinking price. Frontier capability doubles on a schedule, and API pricing falls on a steeper curve. A studio that bets its differentiation on model quality wakes up to find the market has caught up during a single contract cycle.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. A model improvement that lifts output quality by 5 percent is visible to every competitor within weeks. A workflow integration that captures 5 percent more operational data is invisible, proprietary, and compounding.

This is why the evaluation question for vertical AI is not which model the product uses. The question is which workflow the product owns. Ownership means the software sits inside the operation, the operator cannot leave without switching costs, and every run writes data back into the product.

## The Workflow as the Asset

A vertical AI product earns its position by owning the workflow, not by wrapping a model around it. The product must replace the tool the operator uses today, capture the data that flows through the operation, and close the loop by improving the output.

The healthcare vertical demonstrates the pattern. A documentation assistant that only transcribes produces no proprietary asset. A system that sits inside the clinical workflow, routes the documentation, tracks the follow-up, and learns the clinic's referral patterns accumulates a dataset no transcription API can match.

The same logic holds in legal, logistics, and accounting. The winner is not the studio with the best summarization.

The winner is the studio whose software carries the matter, the shipment, or the ledger. The data accrues to the carrier.

## The Compounding Loop

The moat compounds through a loop. The workflow produces data. The data trains the domain behavior.

The improved behavior strengthens the workflow. Each cycle raises the switching cost for the operator and the entry cost for the competitor.

The loop has a unit economic signature. Acquisition cost is paid once per customer.

Data capture runs on every transaction at zero marginal cost. The product improves with usage, which means retention improves with usage, which means the customer base becomes the R&D budget.

This is the capital efficiency story of the vertical studio. The studio does not spend on proprietary model training. It spends on integration depth, and the integration depth pays for itself through the data it captures.

## Valuation in a Non-Linear Market

EBITDA says nothing about an AI infrastructure company, because the value sits in the data asset, not the earnings line. The studio roll-up must be valued on the accumulated workflow data, the retention curve, and the switching cost, not the trailing multiple.

The non-linear reality cuts both ways. A roll-up with fragmented integrations owns no unified dataset and carries a linear valuation. A roll-up that consolidated the workflow before consolidating the companies owns a compounding asset and carries a non-linear one.

The diligence question follows. The buyer must verify that the integration is real, that the data is structured, and that the loop is closed. A studio that acquired ten point solutions without merging their workflows bought revenue, not a moat.

## The Consolidation Order

The order of consolidation decides the outcome. Roll up the workflow first, then the companies. A studio that merges the operations, the data models, and the delivery standards before the legal entities captures the compounding benefit from day one.

The reverse order produces a portfolio of disconnected tools. The studio owns the companies and none of the data, because each company runs its own stack. The roll-up becomes a holding company with a marketing umbrella and no moat.

The studio's edge is the integration machinery. The playbook is to standardize the workflow across the acquired base, migrate the data into a shared schema, and retire the redundant tools. The migration is the value creation event, and the shared schema is the moat.

## The SEK Unit Economics

The capital efficiency math works in SEK. A studio that integrates one workflow standard across ten acquired companies captures the same data volume as a competitor that builds ten separate integrations, at a fraction of the engineering cost.

The acquisition cost per company drops with standardization, because the integration playbook runs once and repeats. The data value per company rises with consolidation, because each company's data strengthens the shared model. The two curves cross early, and the studio that reaches the crossing point first wins the vertical.

The edge case discipline matters here. The studio must model the failure modes: a key customer that refuses the data migration, a vertical where the workflow differs by region, a model that degrades on the long tail. The roll-up economics hold only when the edge cases are priced.

## The Exit Path

The data moat is the exit asset. A strategic buyer acquires the workflow position, not the revenue stream. The purchase price reflects the switching cost of the installed base and the proprietary data, and both are functions of integration depth.

The prepared exit runs the same playbook as the prepared roll-up. The workflows are unified, the data is structured, and the moat is documented before the process starts. The buyer verifies the loop with the same diligence the studio applied to its acquisitions.

The studio that owns the workflow sells the workflow. The studio that owns a collection of tools sells a collection of tools. The difference in multiple is the difference between a data asset and a revenue stream.

## Summary

The data moat decides the vertical AI outcome. Models are commodity inputs, and workflow data is the compounding asset.

Studios win by owning the workflow, closing the data loop, and consolidating the integration before the companies. The moat shows in unit economics, in retention, and in the exit multiple, because the buyer pays for the workflow position, not the model.
