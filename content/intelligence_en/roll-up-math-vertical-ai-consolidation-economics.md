A roll-up is an arithmetic claim. The acquirer asserts that the combined entity is worth more than the sum of its parts, and the assertion must survive integration costs, churn leakage, and the slower growth of acquired units. Vertical AI adds a new variable: the acquired company's data and workflow become inputs to a shared model layer that improves with scale.

The math works when the shared layer is real. It fails when the consolidation is a spreadsheet event and the acquired units keep running as independent businesses with a shared logo. The difference is measurable, and the measurement decides which studio compounds.

## The Unit Economics of Acquisition

The first number is the acquisition multiple. A vertical software company with recurring revenue trades at a multiple of ARR, and the multiple varies by growth, retention, and category position. Per Euclid Ventures' Vertical Report 2026, category-defining vertical SaaS companies compound over long periods, and the report's case study of Clio documents 17 years of compounding before the AI era became a funding catalyst.

The second number is the integration cost. Migrating the acquired company's data model, consolidating its billing, and unifying its workflow layer consumes engineering capacity. The cost is real, and the roll-up math must price it before the acquisition, not after.

The third number is the retention curve of the acquired base. Churn leakage is the silent killer of roll-ups. The acquired customers did not choose the consolidator, and every product change risks a renewal decision. The roll-up that protects the acquired base preserves the multiple it paid.

## The Shared Layer Thesis

The consolidation thesis rests on the shared layer. The acquirer builds one model stack, one workflow engine, and one data schema, and each acquired company feeds the layer with its domain data. The layer improves with every addition, and the improvement lifts every unit in the portfolio.

Per L40's 2026 analysis of AI roll-ups, the serial acquisition model was pioneered before the AI era by firms like Constellation Software, which acquired and held vertical software companies with minimal integration disruption. The AI era adds the shared layer on top of the buy-and-hold model, and the layer is where the compounding lives.

The shared layer changes the acquisition math. A company that adds data and workflow depth to the layer is worth more to the consolidator than to a standalone buyer, because the consolidator monetizes the addition twice: once in the acquired unit and once in the improved layer serving every other unit.

## When the Math Breaks

The math breaks when the shared layer is fiction. The roll-up that acquires companies with incompatible data models, distinct customer bases, and separate product roadmaps inherits integration debt, and the debt compounds faster than the revenue.

The failure signature is predictable. Engineering spends the first year on migrations instead of product, retention dips as the acquired base churns, and the growth rate of the combined entity converges toward the slowest unit. The spreadsheet showed accretion. The P&L shows integration debt.

The second failure mode is valuation. Per the SaaS M&A consolidation data for 2026, private equity dry powder seeking deployment stands near 3 700 billion dollars, roughly 39 000 miljarder SEK, and enterprise CIOs plan vendor consolidation in significant numbers, with one survey reporting 68 percent planning consolidation in 2026. The capital is real, and the competition for quality vertical assets is real. The studio that overpays for a thin shared layer buys the debt, not the thesis.

## The Capital Efficiency Model

The studio model exists because it is capital efficient. A studio builds or acquires vertical AI companies without the overhead of a standalone venture firm, and it monetizes the shared layer across the portfolio. The efficiency shows in the burn multiple: the studio deploys less capital per unit of portfolio value because the layer is built once.

The edge case analysis matters here. The studio that consolidates five healthcare AI point solutions into one platform captures the workflow, the data, and the pricing power. The studio that consolidates five unrelated vertical tools captures a portfolio of small companies with no shared economics. The first is a roll-up. The second is a holding company.

The pricing power is the endgame. A consolidated vertical platform with the leading workflow, the accumulated data, and the switching costs of an embedded product prices like a category leader. The standalone point solutions price like utilities. The gap between the two is the roll-up's exit value.

## The Exit Math

The exit is the proof of the thesis. The consolidated platform exits at a multiple of the category, while the standalone units exit at a multiple of their individual growth. The roll-up converts the spread into enterprise value, and the conversion is the studio's return.

The exit buyer is a strategic or a financial sponsor. The strategic buys the workflow and the data. The sponsor buys the recurring revenue and the consolidation runway. Both pay a premium for the shared layer, because both intend to keep consolidating the category.

The prepared exit requires the same discipline as the acquisition. The data room must document the shared layer's contribution to each unit's economics. The model must show the acquisition math, the integration cost, and the retention curve. The buyer that sees the compounding buys the platform. The buyer that sees the spreadsheet walks.

## Summary

The roll-up math of vertical AI compounds when the shared layer is real. The acquisition multiple, the integration cost, and the retention curve decide the unit economics, and the shared model layer converts the portfolio into a platform. The studio that builds the layer capital-efficiently owns the category's pricing power, and the studio that buys a spreadsheet inherits the debt.
