## Exclusive Dealflow in the Nordics: How We Find Off-Market SaaS at 4x EBITDA

## A New Architecture for Nordic Dealflow

The Nordic SaaS landscape has undergone a structural recalibration over the past five years. On the surface, a market that has become more mature, more homogeneous, and increasingly priced according to international multiples is visible. Beneath the surface, something else is happening: a growing arbitrage window driven by data diffusion, fragmented ownership structures, and technical debt in the companies’ own sales and reporting systems.

What is interesting is that the most attractive transactions are no longer found in traditional processes. They occur in a middle layer of companies that are too mature for venture capital but too early or too operationally messy for larger PE funds. Here, an information gap arises, and in that gap lies the opportunity to find off-market SaaS companies at multiples closer to 3.5x to 5x EBITDA than today’s public benchmarks.

The core question is not whether these companies exist, but how to find them in time. To do this requires not more analysts, but a systematic way to build information infrastructure around private cash flows. This is the foundation for an institutional approach to dealflow in the Nordics, where you do not wait for banks to distribute pitch materials, but construct your own data streams that evaluate, filter, and prioritize signals before the market has time to react.

## Why Off-Market SaaS Is Still Mispriced

Despite increasing sophistication among investors in the region, it is clear that a significant portion of the Nordic SaaS market is still priced as if the companies were local service businesses. There are three main reasons.

First, cash flows in software companies are often more complex than owners realize. Many Nordic SaaS companies in the 40 to 150 million ARR segment lack standardized reporting systems. They can deliver EBITDA figures that are correct in an accounting sense but are difficult to translate into a risk-adjusted value when lacking visibility into churn profiles by cohort, expansion dynamics, or realizability of price increases. This lack of clarity reduces the number of potential buyers, which suppresses multiples.

Second, companies with high product-dependent customer bases are often misclassified. A SaaS that has historically grown 8 to 12% per year can often double its growth rate through better funnel instrumentation, more rigorous success automation, and modernization of outbound layers. This latent growth is not visible in today’s multiples. It exists in the friction of the systems, not in the Excel models.

Third, there is a cultural trait in the Nordics where owners of profitable software companies tend to keep a low profile and avoid banks until an exit is absolutely necessary. This generates structural inefficiency: many of the best companies never enter the market’s pipeline until they have already changed hands informally.

The result is that companies with 20 to 40% EBITDA margins are often traded at multiples that internationally are only given to companies with 8 to 12% margins. This is an arbitrage that arises when internal data structures are weak and external investors lack signal intensity.

## The System Behind Off-Market Arbitrage

Identifying these mispriced companies is not a question of gut feeling or network. It is a question of system design. It is about building a data warehouse that can calibrate, validate, and predict which companies will exhibit structural undervaluation before they themselves realize it.

The architecture typically consists of five layers.

The first is external data collection. This does not involve scraping sensitive information or opaque methods. It is about aggregating public signals that already exist but few use systematically: API logs from integration ecosystems, user behavior on open documentation portals, hiring patterns in technical roles, changes in credit risk models, and frequency of ownership dispositions in holding companies. These data points are fragmented and cannot be understood without a common model.

The second layer is normalization. It is not enough to collect data. It must be filtered, standardized, and weighted against niche dynamics. A company with increased hiring in customer success can indicate expansion as much as it can indicate increasing churn. The ability to distinguish between these interpretations is key.

The third layer is prioritization. This is where the system determines whether a company is an arbitrage candidate or just one of many stable but overvalued SaaS products in the Nordics. The key is to identify mismatches between internal performance and external valuation. A company with high product stickiness, low churn, and intact margins, but with a limited outbound structure, often carries unrealized potential.

The fourth layer is human validation. Even in an infrastructure-driven system, the final assessment must be made by people who understand the industry’s microstructure. The model signals. The team validates. It is a collaboration, not a delegation.

The fifth layer is timing. A well-built dealflow system is not a tool for mass outreach. It is a structure for knowing exactly when a company is in a phase where contact is natural, relevant, and value-creating for both parties. Timing is not soft data. It is a function of seasonality, capital cycles, hiring patterns, and internal changes in owner families.

## Why the Multiple Stays at 4x EBITDA

Many ask why the multiples for Nordic SaaS companies in these segments do not move toward the international standard of 8x to 12x EBITDA. There is not one answer. There are several.

Nordic currencies have historically created inertia in pricing for international actors. Most American and British funds have internal thresholds for currency-risk-adjusted returns. This makes them tend to avoid transactions under €50 million, even if they are fundamentally among the best in the region. This leaves room for more locally anchored actors with technical competence to make deals that international funds do not consider large enough to justify the operational effort.

Furthermore, there is capital concentration in the Nordics where pension money, larger PE funds, and international growth funds focus on the segment of €150 to €1,000 million in enterprise value. Below that level, competition is lower, and multiples remain accordingly.

The third reason is cultural. Many Nordic SaaS founders run their businesses like stable family companies: low costs, high margins, no aggressive expansion. This is not a flaw. It is a strategy. But it affects how the market values them. They appear less scalable than they actually are because they lack the growth engine required to be visible in international screening processes.

## How Data Signals Reveal When the Company Is Ready

One of the most powerful tools in the system is the ability to identify when a company is entering a transitional phase. This often happens long before the company itself realizes it.

Examples of such signals include:

Sudden acceleration in senior hires in product leadership without a corresponding increase in marketing budgets. This indicates internal tension between product organization and commercial realization.

Frequent updates to contract templates and license terms in a short time. This is an indicator that the company is preparing to introduce new pricing levels or change its commercial model.

Increase in user support without a corresponding increase in churn. This suggests the product has gained complex functionality that customers are not fully trained to use. The company is facing a period where structure is needed to convert this complexity into increasing ARPU.

Ownership changes in the holding company. This is one of the most misunderstood signals. Many smaller transfers reflect internal adjustments, generational shifts, or preparations for larger discussions. Timing is critical. When a company is in such a phase, the likelihood that they are considering a transaction is significantly higher.

None of these data points are relevant on their own. It is the intersection of them that drives the precision of a modern dealflow system.

## A New Type of Venture Studio in the Nordics

In international contexts, venture studios are often defined as organizational forms that build companies. In the Nordics, however, a new type of studio is beginning to emerge: a structure that does not only build companies but builds data infrastructure around company building. This means developing tools and models that identify, predict, and enhance value creation in both existing and future portfolio companies.

This is where off-market dealflow and venture building merge. The studio functions as a technical system, not as a traditional investor. Its work is not primarily about finding ideas or teams, but about creating an information advantage that spans the entire region. The system’s role is to offer a more accurate understanding of where software value actually accumulates, not where it happens to be visible on the surface.

This does not mean the process becomes mechanical. On the contrary, it is the combination of human experience and machine precision that generates the results that create returns beyond the market. The model is a filter, not a replacement for intuition. But intuition without data is slower, and slowness is the only thing directly penalized in today’s transaction environment.

## Why the Nordics Is Optimal for This Model

It is not obvious that this type of system would work as well in larger markets. The Nordics has three structural properties that make the region particularly suitable.

The data paradigm in the Nordics is characterized by openness. This means a high amount of structured data that can be used as predictive input without violating privacy or needing opaque methods.

Company structures are often streamlined. There are fewer layers of holding companies, fewer hidden stakeholders, and a high degree of accounting discipline. This makes signals cleaner and analysis easier.

Technical adoption is high. The amount of digital footprints created by both customers and software companies themselves is greater than in almost any other region in the world. This means predictive models can be made more accurate with less historical data.

It is this combination that makes the arbitrage window possible. Not because the Nordics is inefficient, but because it is more transparent and more systematic than other markets. Transparency does not mean efficient pricing. On the contrary, it means that whoever builds the best system around the data gains the advantage.

## Why Off-Market Is Absolutely Critical in the Nordics

In the Nordics, there is a paradox that many international buyers do not understand. The market is both small and extremely high-quality. This creates a situation where good SaaS companies either never reach the market or are sold in silence long before any broader process begins.

The entrepreneurs driving these companies are often engineers, product-focused, and more interested in building than marketing their own company. This leads to actors who only look through formal M&A channels almost always arriving too late. By the time something appears as a listed object, dozens of buyers have already been in and pushed up the multiples.

Off-market is therefore not an option. It is the only way to do deals at attractive multiples in the region.

Our experience is that when you reach an entrepreneur before they have decided to sell, the conversation is fundamentally different. It is about vision, timing, owner fatigue, and what they want to do after the company. Not about maximizing price in an auction. And in the Nordics, trust plays a larger role than price pressure within a certain range.

## How We Built the Nordic Network Over Ten Years

When people hear the word dealflow, they often believe the process starts at the first contact with a company. In reality, our pipeline began to be built much earlier through three separate ecosystems we have been deeply involved in.

Technology clusters in Norway, Sweden, and Denmark where many SaaS companies are born from traditional industries.

Product and data specialists who often know about micro-SaaS projects long before they reach any market.

Founder communities that have a completely different type of information flow than investor circles.

The key here is that the relationships are not built as buyer relationships. Most were created from the start as exchanges about product, technology, and market. We often find out that someone is considering an exit six months before the process even reaches advisors. Often, it is a confidential conversation that begins with a casual comment that energy is running out or that the company is facing a phase that requires something other than the entrepreneur’s strengths.

It is in these early situations we can position ourselves without competition. Acquiring a company is often a direct consequence of many years of developed relationships.

## Data-Driven Screening of Undiscovered SaaS Companies

The second component of our off-market flow is industrial screening. The Nordics is small, but the number of small and medium-sized B2B SaaS companies is larger than most think. We have built a systematic methodology where we map companies based on factors often ignored in traditional databases.

Indicators that suggest stable customer rationality despite low market presence.

Technical choices that signal scalability or particularly high product discipline.

Pricing models that yield strong net cohort even at low growth.

Ownership structures that suggest the entrepreneur is reaching a natural endpoint.

Because many Nordic SaaS companies have never taken capital, they only appear as footnotes in industry registers, often with revenue of SEK 10 to 60 million, stable EBITDA, and extremely low churn. These companies never appear in pitch decks from advisors. They must be discovered manually.

We are talking about companies that have been able to grow profitably for ten years without anyone ever trying to buy them.

## How We Enter Dialogue Before the Process Escalates

Once we have identified a company, the process takes a form that is unusual in classic M&A. We do not enter with a pitch, a letter of interest, or any formal structure. We start by having a neutral conversation about market, product, and where the entrepreneur wants to take the company. The conversation is not sales-oriented. It is curious and open.

It is only when we understand the entrepreneur’s motivation that we even mention the possibility of acquisition. And it is critical to understand what type of entrepreneur you are meeting.

There are those who want to hand over the baton to focus on other projects.

Those who want to offload to have the energy to drive the next step.

Those who want to stay but bring in capital to grow faster.

Those who are mentally done and want to exit without drama.

In the Nordics, entrepreneurs are often risk-aware and value simplicity and integrity far more than aggressive pricing. This means the process can often happen quickly and discreetly as long as trust is in place.

## Why 4x EBITDA Is Possible Off-Market

A common question is why a profitable SaaS company would accept 4x EBITDA when on-market often yields higher multiples.

The explanation is that on-market almost always drives up the price, but it also changes the entire dynamic. In an auction, the seller must:

Prepare detailed documentation.

Manage multiple investors simultaneously.

Risk that processes drag on for months.

Keep the business running in parallel with extensive DD.

This is a high mental price for many Nordic entrepreneurs. The combination of fatigue, focus on simplicity, and desire not to let half the market into their figures makes off-market an attractive alternative.

When the seller experiences that we:

Understand their product.

Offer a smooth process.

Can act quickly.

Do not intend to renegotiate at the last minute.

Then it weighs heavier than potentially getting a higher multiple in a stressful bidding format.

4x EBITDA is therefore not a result of hard negotiation. It is a result of making it easier to say yes.

## Which Companies Are Best Suited for Off-Market

There are specific types of SaaS where off-market with low multiples works particularly well.

B2B SaaS with a niche customer base in traditional industries such as maritime, construction, audit, or energy management.

Companies where the entrepreneur has owned 100% or close to it for a long time and lacks external pressure for maximum exit.

Businesses where growth is low but very stable.

Operations where churn is near zero and customers stay for generations.

It is also clear that companies built in the Scandinavian tradition of technical purity and control are often undervalued compared to more marketing-driven SaaS in the US.

These companies are not built to be acquired. They are built to function. This also makes them significantly more valuable once owned.

## How We Structure the Purchase to Give the Seller Security

An important part of our model is that we do not enter with complex earnout structures. Most entrepreneurs in the Nordics react negatively to this. They do not want to sell a company and then have to work for three years to receive their payout.

Instead, we use structures where:

Payment is primarily upfront.

Any additions are tied to something they can control in the short term.

The seller has the option to choose a role afterward, not forced into one.

Transition happens at their pace.

This makes us win dealflow without always having to win on price.

Security beats premium in Sweden, Norway, and Denmark almost every time.

## What Happens After the Purchase and Why It Creates More Deals

An often underestimated effect of off-market is what happens after the transaction. If the seller feels respectfully treated, experiences a smooth process, and no renegotiation at the last minute, it does not take long before they tell other entrepreneurs in their network.

In the Nordics, reputation is currency. Bad buyers quickly get blocked. Good buyers gain access to the next generation of deals without having to search as hard.

This is precisely why our dealflow grows organically. Every acquisition is effectively marketing for the next.
