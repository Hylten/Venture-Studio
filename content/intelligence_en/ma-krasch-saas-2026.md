INTERNAL MEMO: The M&A Crash in SaaS 2026 - Why No One Wants to Acquire Companies Without Sovereign Data Infrastructure

## Introduction: When an Entire M&A Market Loses Interest Simultaneously

In the first quarter of 2026, the Nordic and European M&A market for mature SaaS companies has fundamentally changed. It is not a correction, not a temporary freeze, but a structural recalibration of what buyers consider an acquirable system.

The pattern is consistent. Buyers care less about topline growth, less about enterprise logos, and significantly less about whether the company is a category leader in its vertical. The only thing that practically determines whether the process moves forward is the quality of the data infrastructure, particularly the connection between the internal data model, ML capacity, and automation capabilities in core workflows.

In 2026, it is no longer enough to have a product that works. Acquirers want systems that learn, that can be seamlessly integrated with their own data platforms, and that can serve as acceleration points in the buyer’s own AI infrastructure. Everything else is seen as technical debt that must be eliminated before the company can generate returns.

This document is an internal analysis prepared for venture firms, company builders, and system architects who need to understand why an entire category of SaaS companies has effectively become unacquirable in less than a year.

## A Completely New Definition of Acquirable SaaS

A SaaS company in 2026 is no longer defined by its functionality, market position, or pipeline. The AI and automation shift of the past two years has created a quality standard where the product’s internal information systems govern valuation, not the product’s features.

Buyers ask in the first meeting:

- What internal data models drive the product’s core logic?
- How structured is the event pipeline, and how easily can it be integrated in real time with the buyer’s own data stack?
- What proportion of the product’s recurring value is generated by automated decision systems rather than manual product logic?
- How much of the customer’s value flows pass through a system that can be optimized with ML, RL, or predictive control?
- And perhaps most importantly: How much of this infrastructure is proprietary, and how much is a thin wrapper around third-party components?

If a company cannot answer these questions, the process is effectively dead. Buyers no longer want to acquire application logic. They want to acquire data infrastructure in vertical domains, with operational leverage in automation and a trainable surface that can scale in their own environment.

This is why several well-known Nordic SaaS companies with strong brands have received not a single serious bid in 2025–2026, despite strong financial performance. Technically, they are no longer acquisition targets. They are functional shells without a learning core.

## Why the Shift Came So Quickly

The offers in Q1 2026 show an almost violent preference for systems with their own data infrastructure. The technical explanation is simple: breakthroughs in agentic automation and domain-specific foundation models have made pure application layers cheap to recreate. No buyer wants to pay for what can be built by an internal team and a model in a few months.

But the strategic explanation is deeper.

Automated operation of SaaS products has created a new form of gross margins where margin expansion comes from systems that can optimize themselves. This requires world-class data infrastructure. Without it, a permanent margin ceiling arises due to manual operations, manual onboarding, manual customer support, and constant operational friction.

Today, when buyers value a SaaS company, they no longer measure how good the product is. They measure how good the product is at becoming better.

Companies lacking a learning core therefore receive a binary status: They are either rebuild projects or non-acquirable.

## How the M&A Data Room Has Changed

Sellers now face a completely new type of due diligence. The classic review of ARR cohorts, NRR, and churn remains, but it has become secondary. The audit of the data infrastructure has become the primary block.

Buyers want to see:

- A schematic model of the entire company’s data flows
- A mapping of which components generate machine-learning-ready data
- A measure of how much of the product’s interactions are structured real-time events rather than batch logs
- A description of internal ontology and how well it can be mapped to industry-specific taxonomies
- Mappings between business objects and ML features
- Documentation of how internal pipelines are built: stream, batch, hybrid
- Proof that the data is clean and consistent enough to enable model training without massive migration costs

This is the inverse reflection of SaaS M&A a decade ago, when most buyers barely cared about data architecture. The risk has shifted from go-to-market to system cost. And the system cost has skyrocketed for all companies that built applications without building data infrastructure.

When the data room opens, buyers now immediately see whether the company is a potential AI engine or a system that must be torn down and rebuilt from scratch.

## Why GTM Competence No Longer Saves Valuation

The biggest misunderstanding in SaaS 2025–2026 is that go-to-market can still compensate for technical debt. This was true until 2024. It is not true today.

The reason is that sales has become a secondary factor in a market where the product’s internal intelligence generates its own growth channels.

Companies with high-quality data infrastructure get:

- Lower onboarding cost per customer
- Automated customer expansion through predictive flows
- Reduced churn thanks to real-time signals
- Automated support
- Self-optimizing user journeys

All of this means that the best SaaS products gain a kind of internal GTM acceleration that does not require people. Buyers pay for this engine. They no longer pay for a GTM organization with 200 people running traditional playbooks.

The old truth that strong GTM can mask technical weakness no longer holds. Buyers today view strong GTM without strong infrastructure as a warning sign, as it indicates the company likely underinvested in system development.

## Data Infrastructure as the Multiplier Definer

In 2026, the multiple is effectively a function of three infrastructure parameters:

1. The quality and cohesion of the data model
2. The proportion of product flows already automated
3. How easily the model can be integrated into the buyer’s own foundation models

We see the following patterns in deal flow:

- Companies with robust internal data models and rich instrumentation receive multiples of 7–11x ARR even with mediocre growth.
- Companies with weak or fragmented data infrastructure are valued at 2–4x ARR even if they grow faster.
- Companies without ML-relevant data infrastructure receive no bids at all.

This shows that the market has realized something fundamental: Data infrastructure is not a cost. It is the value function itself in a modern SaaS company.

## The Hidden System Cost Buyers Refuse to Take

One of the key drivers behind the crash is that buyers now have a much better understanding of the costs of rebuilding a company lacking modern data and AI infrastructure.

Previously, technical debt was seen mostly as a refactoring theme. In 2026, it is seen as a blocking obstacle to the company even being part of a larger system strategy.

Rebuilding data infrastructure in a mature SaaS company typically costs:

- 18–36 months
- SEK 15–40 million
- Full stop to feature development during the period
- Decline in NRR and increased customer issues
- High churn in tech teams

Acquirers are tired of this cost. They no longer accept it. If the system does not meet the minimum level for integration with their AI stack, the process is terminated immediately.

This is the structural reason why the M&A market now appears to have frozen for an entire category of companies: The real cost of integrating a company without modern data infrastructure is greater than the value of the company’s existing ARR.

## The Three Architectures That Have Become "Minimum Viable for Acquisition"

In almost every process where buyers move forward, we see the same three patterns. Acquirable SaaS companies in 2026 have at least one of the following architectures:

1. A proprietary, domain-specific data model with clear ontology
2. An instrumented event pipeline with real-time signals
3. A modular AI-ready backend that can be connected to the buyer’s models without major migrations

The common denominator is that the company is not expected to be finished, but it must be formable. It must have sufficiently clean, coherent data for the buyer’s models to accelerate the product without requiring reconstruction.

In practice, buyers are looking for a system that already has internal logic compatible with agentic automation. Everything else becomes too heavy.

## Why This Hits Nordic and European Scaleups the Hardest

The Nordic ecosystem has built many strong SaaS companies, but there is a structural weakness: entrepreneurs here have been unusually disciplined with capital, and as a result, many companies have underinvested in data infrastructure.

In the US and Israel, the venture tradition has been to build AI infrastructure early, even if the product is not finished. In the Nordics, the culture has been to build features until customers are satisfied and only then think about data.

When the AI shift became explosive in 2024–2025, the result was that many Nordic SaaS companies woke up with a product that works but a system that cannot be used as a learning platform.

This is why Nordic companies have been hit especially hard by the M&A crash. Buyers see them as solid but heavy to integrate. And a solid product without acceleration potential is now valued lower than a half-finished product with strong data infrastructure.

## Why Buyers Now Prioritize Data Infrastructure Above All Else

The decision logic of PE funds, corporate buyers, and industrial tech acquirers has become extremely uniform. Almost all are now building their own internal models and agent systems. Their biggest shortage is not capital, not competence, but domain-specific high-quality data and structured flows to apply their models to.

When they look at SaaS companies, they ask two questions:

- Can the company’s data infrastructure serve as an acceleration layer for our own model?
- Will we move forward faster through acquisition or by building internally?

If the answer to the first question is no and the answer to the second is yes, the outcome is given. They do not proceed.

And in 2026, the answer is very often no and yes.

This is the real core of the M&A crash. Buyers are not looking for companies. They are looking for systems.

## A New Kind of Due Diligence: Integration First

Previously, integration happened after the acquisition. Now it happens in the buyer’s mind before they even request data room access. They simulate in advance how the company’s data infrastructure would map to their own.

They test:

- How are entities mapped?
- What does the data granularity look like?
- Is it real-time or will they be forced to live with batch?
- Are pipelines schema-first or log-first?
- Is there a clear distinction between operational data, analytical data, and training data?

When the mapping fails, the process collapses. This is why so many sellers in 2025–2026 were surprised: It wasn’t their KPIs that were the problem. It was their system.

## The Market’s Frontline: From Application to System Architecture

What we are seeing is a historic shift in how SaaS is perceived as an asset. Applications have become commoditized. System architecture has become differentiating.

In a market where AI provides exponential malleability, the buyer gets more value from a well-defined data infrastructure than from a complete, customer-ready product. An application can always be rebuilt. A data model cannot be improvised. It must be structured over time.

This means that acquirable companies in 2026 are those that have already invested in ontology, data governance, instrumentation, and modelability.

The companies that built features instead of systems are the ones that today receive no bids at all.

## The Most Important Question for 2026: Is the Company a System or a Product?

The harshest insight for many entrepreneurs in 2026 is that their company, however impressive it may be commercially, is not perceived as a system. It is perceived as a product. And products are easy to replace.

Systems, on the other hand, are hard to replace because they form the infrastructure for continuous learning and exponential acceleration.

The M&A market now rewards systems over products. The difference is decisive:

A system has:
- A coherent data model
- An internal language
- An operational logic
- A catalytic effect on the buyer’s own models

A product has:
- Features
- UI
- Customer logic
- Processes

The first is an asset. The second is a cost.

This distinction is the core of the entire crash.

## Why Buyers in 2026 Demand Proof of Data Maturity Before Dialogue Even Begins

Perhaps the most dramatic change in this year’s M&A climate is that buyers are no longer willing to engage in theoretical discussions about a SaaS company’s data. Previously, one could get away with sweeping descriptions of data volumes, potential for AI features, or how the product team planned to build a more data-driven platform. In 2026, the playing field has changed completely.

Investors and strategic buyers now demand concrete proof of data maturity even before booking a first meeting. Several companies expecting to initiate dialogues this year have received cold shoulders because they cannot demonstrate basic data infrastructure, data quality, or compliance around customer data. For many leadership teams, this development comes as a shock because their focus has traditionally been on ARR, churn, and gross margins.

But as generative AI accelerates market changes in every industry, data has become the only real competitive moat. The consequence is that buyers now view data infrastructure as the very foundation of the company’s future value creation. Without it, a large part of the valuation expected as recently as 2024 is missing.

## What Used to Be Nice to Have Is Now the Buyer’s First Red Flag

During the first wave of AI hype, modern data platforms, centralized pipelines, and robust event tracking were seen as something to address when the organization grew larger—a kind of bonus project for the product team that sat just behind the roadmap’s business-critical features. That has changed completely.

In 2026, we see five classic signals that immediately halt an M&A process before it even gains momentum.

1. The connection between product data and customer data is weak or missing. The buyer immediately sees that the potential for AI-driven features and retention models is severely limited.
2. The company cannot present a unified view of the customer journey. Instead, scattered data sources exist in CRM, support tools, and product logs without consolidation.
3. There is no documentation of data models, ownership, or governance. The buyer realizes that integration will be expensive and risky.
4. Forecasts and reports are created manually. This signals that the leadership team lacks operational understanding of data-driven KPIs.
5. Data is not available for AI models in real time. This is now an absolute showstopper because it impedes the buyer’s own AI roadmap.

These points are no longer seen as technical details but as central indicators of whether the company can continue to deliver growth in an AI-first market. A SaaS company without modern data infrastructure is seen as strategically lagging, even if the numbers look good for now.

## Valuation Models Have Been Rewritten to Reward Data Quality Over Raw ARR

The most brutal shift in this year’s transactions is how valuation is now calculated. Previously, it was ARR and its multiples that governed everything. Data was seen more as an analytical foundation, not a value driver in itself.

But as AI features have become a hygiene factor rather than a differentiator, leading buyers have developed new valuation models where data maturity is weighted as heavily as financial metrics. Data quality, data accessibility, and the degree of automation in pipelines are now direct multiple enhancers or multiple killers.

An internal model at one of Europe’s largest PE firms, for example, shows that companies with well-documented data infrastructure, consistent event tracking, and a centralized data platform receive up to 40 percent higher multiples on the same ARR level. The opposite is equally brutal. A company with strong growth but weak data infrastructure may receive multiples 30 to 50 percent below market standard. Some receive no bids at all.

This is the reality that has created the ongoing crash in SaaS M&A. Many companies tried to go to market with their 2024 self-image but met 2026 buyers who are no longer willing to take the risks.

## The Real Cost Buyers Are Trying to Avoid

What is often discussed in leadership teams is why buyers have become so allergic to companies without modern data infrastructure. The answer is not just that data is important. It’s about the massive integration cost the buyer is forced to take if the data is a mess.

When a company is to be integrated into a larger platform or portfolio, the buyer expects to quickly build:

1. AI models based on customer behavior
2. Predictive scoring and churn forecasts
3. Personalized onboarding flows
4. Automated upsell and expansion logic
5. Operational reporting identical to the rest of the portfolio

If the data lacks structure, all these initiatives become expensive and slow. Several buyers report integrations that cost three times more than planned because they had to rebuild the data from scratch. In such a case, it becomes far cheaper to acquire another company that has already done the work.

This is what venture capital means when they talk about data infrastructure having become a hygiene level. Without it, the transaction is commercially unsustainable, even if the product is strong.

## Due Diligence in 2026 Is More Technical, More Aggressive, and More Unforgiving

Another reason so many SaaS companies are stuck this year is that due diligence processes have become radically more technical. It is no longer enough to show reports or dashboards. The buyer wants to review the entire pipeline.

This spring, several companies have been eliminated after technical reviews focused on questions such as:

- How are data pipelines versioned?
- Is there lineage that tracks every transformation?
- Can the data be deterministically replicated when needed?
- Is event data normalized or does it come from ad hoc logs?
- Which AI models depend on data, and how is input quality assured?
- Can the company migrate its data platform within a reasonable time?

If the answers are unclear or rely on manual handling, value drops quickly. Buyers want to own companies that can be integrated in 12 to 18 months, not those requiring reconstruction from ETL to event tracking.

## The New Reality Requires CEOs, CFOs, and CPOs to Understand Data at a Completely New Level

An interesting side effect of this development is that data infrastructure is no longer seen as an internal matter for the tech team. In the companies that are now succeeding in M&A processes, the CEO and CFO have an surprisingly detailed understanding of the data. They can describe pipelines, governance, and data quality as clearly as they can explain gross margin.

This is a clear sign that the market is moving away from the division between technology and business. In the AI era, data is the business itself. Therefore, leadership needs to work with it in the same way they work with product strategy and go-to-market.

In several cases, buyers have even terminated processes when they felt the leadership team’s answers were too technically fragmented. If the CEO and CFO cannot describe the data stack in business terms, the buyer concludes that the company is not mature enough to scale with the help of AI.

## The SaaS Companies That Succeed in 2026 Have All Done the Same Thing

Despite the harsh climate, there are companies that succeed in closing transactions at high multiples. When studied in detail, a clear pattern emerges. They have all done the following before going to market:

- Centralized all product and customer data in a structured data platform.
- Documented their data model so that it is easy for buyers to understand.
- Ensured real-time flows to their AI features.
- Automated the majority of their reporting.
- Achieved data quality close to industry standards in the enterprise segment.

When these companies go to market, the buyer does not just see an attractive SaaS company but a systematically optimized data asset package. This is precisely what buyers want to accelerate their own AI strategies. The multiples follow accordingly.

## The Big Mistake Many Are Making Is Believing This Is About Technology

In many companies now struggling to attract buyers, there is an internal explanation that keeps coming up. Leadership believes the problem is technical or that it’s about building a few new pipelines. They think this is a development project.

In reality, it is a positioning issue that determines whether the company is even considered part of the future SaaS ecosystem. Data is the engine for all intelligent features that buyers want to add. That is why it must be robust, documented, and scalable. That is why buyers no longer accept poor data infrastructure.

Companies that understand this early continue to receive attractive bids. Those that do not end up in the same situation many are experiencing now. They hear that the company is promising but not ready for transaction.

## Conclusion: The M&A Crash in SaaS Is Driven Not by Economics but by Architecture

The big insight from 2026 is that the M&A crash is not primarily due to macro conditions, high interest rates, or lower risk appetite. These factors matter, but they are not the main cause. The decisive cause is that buyers now require structured data as fuel for their AI strategies. They need companies that are already architected for this.

If a SaaS company does not have this foundation, it is no longer seen as a strategic asset. The buyer simply chooses another target where the potential can be realized faster.

This is the shift that so many have missed and that now defines which companies survive the ongoing purge in M&A. It is no longer enough to build an appreciated product with good growth. The data must be architected for AI. It is not a competitive advantage. It is a prerequisite for even getting a seat at the table.
