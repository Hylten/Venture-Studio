## Exit Multiples in a Fragmented Market: What Are You Worth?

## Fragmentation Defining Today’s Multiples

Exit multiples in B2B SaaS have traditionally been seen as a function of ARR quality, growth, and gross margins. During the 2010s and early 2020s, a relatively homogeneous market view dominated: if two companies had similar metrics, their multiples would be close. That era is effectively over.

Today’s market for valuing software companies has fragmented to such a depth that multiples no longer reflect an industry standard but instead an increasingly granular matrix of data points around:

- Infrastructure dependencies
- Go-to-market architecture
- Capital intensity and margin structure
- AI differentiation and model costs
- Risk profile according to the buyer’s system stack
- Data sovereignty and distribution control

This creates a market where two companies with identical ARR, identical growth, and identical churn can differ by up to 5x in exit multiple, depending on how well their architecture and data flows fit into the buyer’s strategic system.

Multiples have therefore moved from being the result of financial performance metrics to being the result of structural compatibility metrics. For an entrepreneur at Series C and beyond, this means a paradigm shift: you are no longer primarily optimizing for topline or burn, but for how acquirable your system architecture is.

It is in this changed context that the question of what a SaaS company is actually worth needs to be reformulated from the ground up.

## From Financial Story to Systemic Story

Historically, SaaS was valued based on a narrative of recurring revenue, growth potential, and sticky usage. But today, when software is seen as a component in larger data loops, a pure financial story is no longer sufficient.

Buyers, especially strategic ones, now seek:

- Locked distribution positions through API flows
- Ownership over critical data pipelines
- Advantages in model training and inference optimization
- Systems that reduce their own infrastructure costs
- Components that strengthen their internal data supply chain

This means a product previously judged by functionality is now valued based on how it integrates into an increasingly complex AI-driven ecosystem. In this logic, a company’s value is not a function of what it sells, but what type of node it is in a chain-spanning system.

This is why multiples diverge. They are no longer valuing the same thing. They are valuing two different types of assets: feature-level software vs. system-level software.

## Infrastructure Dependencies as Value Drivers and Valuation Risks

A company’s dependencies practically define the upper limit of its exit multiple. In a world where inference costs, latency, and data mobility affect gross margins as much as sales costs, infrastructure choices are not technical decisions but valuation decisions.

Companies deeply embedded in hyperscaler dependencies or model dependencies struggle to reach premium multiples because:

- Margin expansion is structurally blocked by third-party pricing
- Cost models are volatile and unpredictable
- Business models conflict with the hyperscaler’s own expansion plans
- Integration with the buyer’s existing systems becomes expensive and slow

Conversely, companies that have:

- Self-controlled data infrastructure
- Hybrid cloud readiness
- Modular architecture for inference routing
- Ability to adapt model choices to the customer’s compute stack

...are valued higher because they not only reduce risk at acquisition but can also increase the value of the buyer’s existing systems. Their multiple moves from a discount logic to an enhancement logic.

This is where today’s multiples begin to diverge significantly.

## Data as Strategic Inventory Rather Than an Asset

In the early SaaS years, data was considered one of the most central assets for valuation. But this was a simplified view based on the assumption that:

- Data was hard to copy
- Data was hard to replace
- Data enabled unique model advantages

In a situation where model marginal utility is decreasing and data flows are becoming more standardized, it is no longer the volume of data that drives value, but:

- Data portability
- Data structure
- Data’s role in external pipelines
- Degree of control over data collection flow
- How data stabilizes inference and tuning in real-time systems

This shift is why some companies with large datasets are valued low, while some companies with relatively small datasets are valued extremely high. It is not the data itself that is valuable, but its function in the acquiring actor’s system architecture.

## AI Layer, Model Costs, and the Multiples Gap That Continues to Grow

AI introduces additional dimensions in valuation. Today, acquirers analyze:

- How high are model costs and to what degree do they scale with the customer base?
- Is the model self-trained, fine-tuned, or entirely dependent on third parties?
- What risks exist for operational disruptions tied to model providers?
- How much data triangulation is needed for reliable output?
- Can the system run with alternative models to lower cost or latency?

Companies that cannot control their inference stack are perceived as feature layers, not system layers. This can mean multiples in the range of 2x to 6x ARR, regardless of growth.

Companies that, on the other hand, have their own training pipeline, model architecture that can be optimized for specific customer needs, or unique data loops receive entirely different multiples. For system layers, multiples can reach 10x to 20x, sometimes higher with strategic synergies.

This is not a hype effect but a structural consequence of how AI is changing the value chain. For a buyer, it is simply more expensive not to own the system layer than to pay a high multiple to consolidate it.

## Convergence of Product, Distribution, and Data Flows

A central reason for multiple divergence is that SaaS products today are often distribution layers as much as software. They function as data points in a chain where customer interactions provide the buyer with:

- Signals
- Labels
- Process data
- Domain-specific inputs for model optimization

Whoever owns the distribution layers owns control over the data’s origin, rhythm, and quality. This makes distribution positions significantly more valuable than technical functionality.

This also means multiples rise sharply when a company:

- Has technically difficult integrations that are structural for the customer’s business
- Sits on data flows that are expensive to recreate
- Owns a unique contact point in the customer’s internal processes
- Can function as an injection point for AI into the customer’s systems

Companies without this type of distribution remain generic tools. And generic tools, regardless of revenue, receive generic multiples.

## Acquirability as Its Own Metric and Why It Now Overrides All Traditional KPIs

In a fragmented market, acquirability becomes a central variable. It is a function of:

- How easily you can integrate into the buyer’s system
- How much you reduce the buyer’s own cost
- How much strategic enhancement you provide to the buyer’s position
- How quickly you reduce the buyer’s technical debt
- How well your architecture harmonizes with their data flow

It is no longer sales talk that determines value, but a friction map. How many of the buyer’s existing systems need to be reconfigured to fit yours? How much infrastructure needs to be replaced? How much model costs do you add? What regulatory dependencies do you create?

A company with $15 million in ARR can be more acquirable than a company with $60 million, and therefore receive a higher multiple. This is the reality in a landscape where strategy, technology, and data converge in valuation.

## The New Multiple Landscape: Four Archetypes

In a market where multiples are highly fragmented, four clear categories are beginning to crystallize. Even if no two companies are identical, the majority can be placed into one of the following archetypes:

1. Generic SaaS tools
   Low differentiation and strong dependency on third-party infrastructure. Multiples 2x to 5x.

2. Vertical navigation layers
   Niche systems that own critical data pipelines in specific processes. Multiples 6x to 10x.

3. Distribution-owning system layers
   Control important customer interfaces and integrate deeply into business-critical flows. Multiples 10x to 20x.

4. Strategic AI infrastructure components
   Reduce model costs, improve data quality, or enhance the buyer’s AI stack. Multiples 15x to 40x depending on synergy.

Fragmentation is not in the variation of multiples, but in the fact that companies are no longer clustered in a common middle field. They are structurally diverging because their system roles are diverging.

## System Architecture as the Value Standard

At Series C and beyond, valuation becomes primarily a function of architecture. A buyer no longer primarily looks at:

- Your revenue
- Your growth
- Your churn
- Your costs

But at:

- Your role in their system
- Your ability to lower their infrastructure costs
- Your potential to improve their AI models’ output
- Your position in the data loop
- Your control over distribution and data points
- Your dependency on external providers

In this perspective, the exit multiple is a consequence of architecture, not financial performance. Therefore, value is no longer formed by how hard you grow, but how structurally you build.

## When Value Is Determined Before Series C

What makes today’s market particularly demanding is that the factors driving multiples are largely determined before a company reaches Series C. System choices made in Series A can impact exit value later more than the growth curve can compensate for.

Architecture locks in or unlocks multiple potential. If the company was built for a world where horizontal SaaS was still the norm, it may be difficult to convert into today’s system-centric logic. The reverse is equally true: companies built for deep integration, controlled pipelines, and modular AI layers have multiple potential that is harder to destroy.

This is why an increasing number of investors are beginning to examine not just traction, but:

- Structure in data flows
- Degree of ownership in distribution points
- Modularity of model architecture
- Long-term scalability of infrastructure costs
- Depth of system integration

These factors are not cosmetic. They drive 80% of the multiple difference in today’s market.

## Buyers’ Changing Behavior

Buyers in the SaaS sector now act more like system architects than portfolio managers. They ask questions like:

- Which of our internal chains does this strengthen if we acquire it?
- Which cost structures decrease directly?
- Which dependencies disappear and which are added?
- How quickly can we integrate this into our own model pipeline?
- How close is their data to our own de facto standard?

These are questions that replace classic M&A questions about EBITDA, revenue mix, and CAC payback. It also means that companies previously typical private equity targets today instead become strategic targets if their system role is clear enough.

Fragmentation is therefore not a result of chance, but a result of buyers themselves changing their internal logic. And this logic is on track to become the standard for the entire ecosystem.

## The Next Phase: Valuation as a Function of Interoperability

What is emerging is a market where interoperability may be the most important driver of value. As AI systems become more modular, the importance increases for software companies to:

- Connect to different compute and model architectures
- Adapt to multiple data standards
- Abstract away dependencies that would otherwise create technical friction
- Move between clouds and on-prem environments without performance loss

Systems that can connect to multiple architectures receive better multiples than systems tightly bound to one provider or model. This is especially clear in industries like finance, logistics, enterprise SaaS, and industrial systems where data interfaces are not trivial to harmonize.

In practice, this means multiples increasingly reflect how easily the buyer can absorb the system into their own stack. A highly interoperable product can reduce integration time from months to weeks, which in turn can significantly increase the multiple.

It is in this context that the question of value in an exit can no longer be reduced to a multiple of ARR. The multiple is a summary of a much deeper structural analysis, where every component in your system weighs as heavily as your financial KPIs.

## How Multiples Are Formed in a Market Without Clear Direction

When the market is split and buyers look in different directions, there is no longer a single valuation logic that dominates. Instead, multiples are created in a kind of tug-of-war between buyers’ risk perception, capital availability, and the entrepreneur’s ability to demonstrate a credible future path.

In such an environment, a phenomenon many sellers experience as frustrating arises. One buyer may think the company is worth 12x EBITDA, while another thinks it is worth 6x. Same company, same numbers, entirely different perspectives.

The explanation lies in how buyers are now forced to sort in a world where the future can no longer be extrapolated from the past. Multiples therefore become an expression of the buyer’s expectations for the value journey the company can make in their hands. Not what the company is worth today, but what it can become.

This shift means that multiples should no longer be seen as a market price, but as an offer from a buyer to share a future journey.

## The New Normal Range

It is still possible to talk about a normal range, but it is broader and more dependent on niche than before. What was previously a trivial question of industry practice has now become a question of business model, scalability, customer mix, owner role, and how clearly the company can demonstrate a pattern of repeatability.

For most private companies with stable profitability, the range in today’s market is somewhere between 6x and 10x EBITDA. But variations can be much greater. An IT company with subscription revenue may reach 12x to 16x. A traditional company with heavy operational owner role may reach 4x to 7x.

What was previously an exception has now become the standard. The range is wider, uncertainty is greater, and therefore the value of the expected future journey is greater than before. Buyers want to pay for security, scalability, and what is already solved. Sellers are paid for what they have already proven, not for what they could do.

## Multiples No Longer Follow GDP, But Micro Markets

An important change in recent years is that the link between the broad economy and valuations has decreased. During the last decade, multiples largely followed general economic sentiment. High growth meant high multiples, low growth meant lower multiples.

Today, it is entirely different. A company in a micro-niche with stable demand can receive very high multiples even if the macroeconomy is weak. At the same time, a company in a pressured niche can receive low multiples despite good figures.

This means it is less relevant to talk about the business cycle and more relevant to talk about local competition, customer buying behavior, and how dependent the company is on single market sources.

The micro market now drives more than the macro market.

## Buyers’ New Filter: Risk, Time, and Transformation

Buyers’ risk assessment has become more sophisticated. Instead of a traditional due diligence focused on numbers and agreements, a deeper analysis is now made of the company’s ability to develop under new ownership.

Three questions dominate.

1. How much risk remains in the business
2. How long does it take to realize potential
3. How much transformation is required

These three factors significantly impact the multiple. A company that has already completed its transformation, for example digitalization, professionalization, or scalability of the offering, is paid for this.

A company where the buyer must perform the transformation after acquisition receives a lower multiple, simply because the risk and time investment are greater.

This is why it is not uncommon for two companies with similar results to end up with entirely different multiples depending on how much future work the buyer must put in to realize the value.

## The Importance of the Deal Being Repeatable

A word now frequently used in transaction discussions is repeatability. Buyers want to see that the company can generate future revenue without having to reinvent the wheel each time.

This is about:

- How systematic sales are
- How dependent revenue is on key individuals
- How predictable the flow of new customers is
- How much the business is affected if the owner leaves
- How clear the offering is to the market

The more repeatable a company is, the higher multiple it can receive. This is often the most direct path from a multiple of 6 to a multiple of 9. It is not about increasing revenue, but about showing that revenue is predictable and can grow without the organization breaking.

## Fragmentation Creates New Winner Categories

When the market no longer moves in a common rhythm, new categories of companies emerge that are rewarded with high multiples, often higher than would have been possible just a few years ago. These categories have three common traits.

1. They are independent of the business cycle
2. They have limited competition and high customer loyalty
3. They have a clearly scalable business model

Examples include companies in narrow B2B niches, companies that own a specific skill hard to replicate, or companies with strong subscription models.

These companies can receive multiples far above market average, not because they grow fast, but because they are stable, predictable, and hard to replace.

## Multiples Are Higher for Companies That Don’t Need the Seller

A clear and often underestimated factor in valuation is how dependent the company is on the owner. This becomes even more important in a fragmented market where buyers want to minimize risk.

When the owner is central to sales, customer relationships, or operational management, this negatively impacts the multiple. The buyer then sees a risk that value will fall if the owner leaves. This means the multiple reflects not only the company’s potential but also how much of that potential sits in the owner’s head.

Companies that have already professionalized their organization, implemented structural capital, and built a team that can drive the business forward receive higher multiples.

This is one of the clearest differences between companies that receive 6x and companies that receive 10x.

## Price for Proof, Not for Dreams

Buyers today primarily pay for what is already proven. Growth in the pipeline, leads that should become customers, or initiatives that could double revenue count to a much lesser degree.

The reason is that market fragmentation has created a more cautious attitude. Buyers want to see that growth has actually been realized, not just planned.

This means the next major value increase often lies in operationalizing potential before the process starts. When a company wants to maximize its multiple, it should therefore not be sold with promises of future growth, but with actual results already in motion.

This is a shift that makes the right timing often more important than the right narrative.

## Strategic Buyers and Their Multiple Logic

For strategic buyers, the multiple logic has changed even more. They are not primarily looking for cash flow. They are looking for synergies, skills, capabilities, and market position.

The multiple therefore becomes a function of how quickly the acquisition can create a stronger position in the market. A company may be worth 8x in traditional valuation, but 14x for a buyer who can immediately add the offering to their network and increase revenue within a year.

This is important to understand. For some buyers, the multiple is just a mathematical model to explain their strategy. Not a market price. This difference is often invisible to sellers but can be worth tens of millions.

## Private Equity Looks for Machines, Not Projects

Private equity actors have been entrusted with large amounts of capital that still needs to be deployed. This has led to a more selective but still active buyer landscape. For them, valuation is not primarily about today’s results but about how efficiently the company can be run in their model.

They look for companies that:

- Already have a replaceable organization
- Have clear KPIs that can be improved
- Can grow through acquisitions or geography
- Have a business that can be standardized

Multiples therefore become higher for companies that resemble machines and lower for companies that resemble projects.

This is a reality that entrepreneurs often underestimate, because it requires a different way of thinking about governance, processes, and reporting long before entering a sales process.

## So What Are You Worth in Today’s Market?

The answer is not a multiple. It is a range. And the range is determined by three factors.

1. Repeatability
2. Independence from the owner
3. Scalability of the offering

When these three factors are high, the value falls into the upper part of the range. When any of them is missing, the value begins to slide downward. It is not about being perfect, but about showing the company can continue to grow even without the entrepreneur’s daily involvement.

In a fragmented market, value is more tied to future security than historical success. Those who want to maximize their multiple therefore need to create security and repeatability before the sales process begins.
