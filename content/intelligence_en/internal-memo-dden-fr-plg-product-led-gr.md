INTERNAL MEMO: The Death of PLG in Enterprise - Why Agents Kill the Classic Sales Funnel

## Introduction

Something fundamental is happening in enterprise SaaS that has not yet been captured by the industry’s public narrative. Product-led growth (PLG), the former holy grail for Series C+ companies, is losing its system value. It is not about tactical weaknesses or poor execution. It is a structural collapse. A module in the go-to-market architecture that previously functioned with friction as its primary driving force is now unraveling as friction disappears.

The entry of agents into the core operational flows of the system is not only changing how users interact with the system but also how value is measured, how adoption occurs, and how demand is initiated. PLG was a response to a world where the user needed guidance, proof-by-usage, and gradual exposure to functionality. In an agent-driven ecosystem, it is the model itself that drives the user. The classic sales funnel, in both its self-serve and enterprise versions, becomes a relic from a time when software was a tool and not an actor.

This is an internal memo aimed at system builders. To organizations that have already passed the initial scaling steps and now face a new structure where AI agents control data circulation, automate decision logic, and effectively create their own user journeys. For these players, PLG is no longer an acceleration engine but an anachronistic obstacle.

## How PLG Arose as System Logic

To understand the collapse, you must understand what PLG actually solved as a fundamental problem. PLG replaced an era when sales organizations bore the entire cost of explaining the product. By allowing users to onboard themselves, a SaaS product could scale without a marginal cost per new customer. The structure was built on the user taking the initiative. They discovered the tool, imported data, explored features, and established their own habits. When adoption increased, the company charged for deeper functionality or more users.

The central aspect was that PLG shifted the energy cost of discovery to the user. Discovery was human, slow, and individual. It could not be automated. It could, however, be scaled by reducing friction in the product’s interface. PLG was simply a response to the economy of friction.

But that economy no longer applies.

## How Agents’ Logic Breaks PLG’s Core Assumptions

When autonomous or semi-autonomous agents become the core of the product’s value delivery, the user is no longer the pilot. The agent performs the work. The agent initiates processes. The agent drives the flow of information. The agent connects systems with other systems. This means that the entire PLG theory is inverted. Instead of the user driving adoption, the model drives adoption.

An agent-led product does not need to be onboarded by the user. It onboards itself by connecting to data sources, analyzing context, and initiating workflows. It does not need proof-by-usage. It generates proof-by-output. This means that the steps PLG built its existence on are no longer meaningful. Friction has not only been reduced; it has been abstracted away entirely.

This makes the classic self-service funnel irrelevant, but also the enterprise-adapted variant where users were supposed to "feel the value" before scaling. Value is now the first thing that happens, because agents produce value as soon as they receive data. The sales funnel loses its function when output precedes adoption.

## Enterprise Buying Behavior Changes on the Systems Side

In enterprise contexts, it is common for organizations to invest in software before they understand how it will be used. There has always been a structural tension between procurement and usage. PLG was a partially successful solution because it forced adoption to occur before expansion. For the first time in a long time, value and pricing synchronized toward actual behaviors.

But when agents become operational actors, this relationship shifts again. The difference this time is that friction cannot be re-established, and the agent’s output becomes the primary value, not user activity. The system-driven teams, often within data, operations, and product, begin purchasing agent capacity rather than software. Enterprise customers are no longer looking at interfaces, features, or user flows. They are looking at how quickly an agent can integrate with their own data network and what level of autonomy can be allowed without risk.

This means that PLG flows are no longer relevant for capturing enterprise buyers’ attention. They do not want to trial a user interface. They want to test an agent in their own data environment. These are two entirely different things. The traditional PLG signals, such as daily usage, activated flows, or expansion through user groups, become noise. They measure human engagement in a model where humans are no longer doing the work.

## Expansion Shifts from Bottom-Up to Inside-Out

Historically, SaaS expansion has been built on more users organically connecting. A team started, more teams followed, and eventually organization-wide adoption emerged. It was a bottom-up logic that worked as long as human behavior was the bottleneck.

In an agent world, the bottleneck is instead data access and workflow permissions. When an agent is established in one part of the organization, it can quickly expand its own operational scope if it gains more permissions. Expansion is no longer bottom-up, but inside-out. It moves from core systems to peripheral system logic, not from individuals to groups.

This means that the traditional PLG pipeline becomes an incorrect model for value measurement. Expansion no longer occurs because more people see the value, but because the model can extend its operational surface. This is a completely different architecture for adoption, and it is incompatible with PLG thinking.

## PLG’s Data Model Collapses in the Agent Economy

PLG is built on a data model where the product’s own events are used to measure value and detect when sales teams should be activated. But in an agent-led world, the majority of value is generated outside the application. The agent works in external systems, in the customer’s own pipelines, in their databases, their communication layers, and their internal document structures.

This means that core value is not visible in the app’s analytics layer. PLG instrumentation loses its function because the work has moved to a place where PLG was never designed to measure. An agent that automates a business-critical workflow generates enormous value, but from the product’s perspective, it may appear as low activity. It is inverted telemetry. The more value the agent creates, the less visibility the product team has in its own event stream.

If value can no longer be measured with PLG instrumentation, PLG cannot drive PLG as a growth engine. The system is fundamentally broken.

## Agents’ Operational Autonomy Eliminates the Need for PLG Features

Many core PLG features lose their relevance entirely when agents become primary users.

Onboarding flows are not used because agents initiate the processes themselves.

Guided product journeys become meaningless because agents do not need guidance.

Feature discovery as a mechanism for expansion disappears because the agent itself identifies which features it needs.

Usage scoring becomes irrelevant because the user is not the one driving activity.

This means that much of the PLG toolbox is not just reduced in value but becomes redundant. It may remain for human fallback flows, but it no longer drives core business. Enterprise will not invest heavily in products where a significant portion of functionality is designed for user behavior that no longer exists in core flows.

## PLG as Culture Becomes a Burden

PLG is not just a go-to-market model. It is a cultural structure. It is a product philosophy that values minimalism, gradual discovery, and user-triggered value. This culture works poorly when the system needs to build agent ecosystems, autonomy, complex integration layers, and advanced data processing.

In an agent-dominated product, value is a function of data infrastructure, model performance, and integration depth. This is system-building, not experience optimization. The natural impulse of PLG culture, to reduce complexity for the user, conflicts with building the type of complexity an agent needs to act autonomously. This leads to organizational friction where product teams cling to a model that no longer drives the business, while data and agent teams try to scale new architectures.

It is simply impossible to drive an agent ecosystem with a PLG cultural core. The two logics are incompatible.

## Capacity Selling Replaces User-Based Expansion

It is becoming increasingly clear that enterprise will not pay for users when it is agents driving workflows. They will pay for capacity. This is a return to infrastructure logic, but with new dimensions tied to model training, token economics, data movement, and agent autonomy.

This means that PLG methods based on user-based expansion models become irrelevant. Companies do not care about how many people log in. They care about how many processes can be automated, how many systems the agent can interact with, and how much compute power is needed to keep the agent operational in real time.

It is a capacity market. Not a user market.

## Why This Is Not an Evolution of PLG, But Its Endpoint

There is a narrative temptation to describe this as PLG 2.0. That is wrong. PLG was a model built for human behavior optimization in interface-heavy applications. The agent world is built on autonomous system logic with minimal user interaction. PLG cannot evolve into that. PLG no longer solves the right problem.

This means that enterprise companies that continue to invest in PLG infrastructure risk locking capital on the wrong side of a paradigm shift. They cling to funnel optimization when they should be building agent architecture. They analyze user journeys when they should be analyzing data flows. They optimize onboarding when they should be optimizing autonomy.

PLG is not under transformation. It is over.

## What Replaces PLG Is Not SLG, But ALG

Much of the public discussion assumes that sales-led models will return when PLG loses power. That is a flawed assumption based on the idea that software is still bought as software. But agent products are not software. They are operational system resources. They are closer to data architecture than product experience.

Therefore, a new model is emerging: Agent-Led Growth (ALG).

Here, it is the agent’s integrated output, not the user’s behavior, that drives expansion. The agent functions as both product, distribution, and proof. It is a model built on the idea that autonomy leads to demand. It requires a completely different organization, completely different instrumentation, completely different GTM tools, and completely different financing models.

In an ALG system, the product is no longer something that is distributed. It is something that deploys itself, seeks out new workspaces, and initiates its own value chain. PLG is structurally incompatible with this.

## Why This Memo Is Written Now

We are in a time where many enterprise SaaS companies are trying to integrate agents on top of their PLG structures. This creates false stability. The first user gains are positive. The first automations work. But beneath the surface, a system collision is occurring where the PLG infrastructure in the product no longer supports the value the company delivers.

This memo is written to clarify that it is not enough to add agents. You must decommission PLG as the primary model and replace it with a structural architecture for agent-driven operational systems.

It is not a cosmetic update. It is a decommissioning of an entire go-to-market logic, its tools, its culture, and its system assumptions.

And it is precisely in the transition that the greatest opportunities arise for companies willing to release the logic before it collapses under its own weight.

## When PLG Is No Longer Self-Sustaining

For much of the 2010s and 2020s, PLG was considered the holy grail of SaaS. Companies invested enormous sums in onboarding flows, self-learning dashboards, and data analysis to get the user to drive their own buying journey. The idea was that the product itself would be the seller, marketer, and customer success team in one.

But the reality in 2026 is radically different. Companies that previously saw PLG as their most important engine now describe how their user base continues to grow, but revenue does not follow. The classic expansion from free tier to premium, or from team to enterprise, has become uneven, fragmented, and increasingly unpredictable.

It is not the users who have changed. It is the system around them.

What was once the core of PLG no longer works as the sole catalyst because decision-making in companies has shifted to agent-driven structures. Human users remain as operators but not as commercial engines. This changes incentives and the entire buying journey.

## Agents Take Over the Role of Qualifiers

In traditional PLG logic, the user should discover value, activate, and then drive their own expansion inward through the organization. But in 2026, that does not work. Instead, it is the company’s internal agents that determine whether a product should be scaled, integrated, or confined to the sandbox.

Companies have created internal rules where agents act as gatekeepers. They analyze risk, cost, compatibility, and policies before a product is even allowed to be used for real. The onboarding flow, which PLG previously lived and died by, has become secondary. It is the agents’ evaluation that counts.

This means that:

• The product must be able to talk directly to the agent, not just the user
• Features must be machine-readable
• Documentation must be structured for agent adaptation
• API quality has become more important than UI

A product can gain thousands of new human users without a single upgrade. It can also gain zero users but quickly move to enterprise if the agent deems it strategically valuable.

This logic is impossible to capture in the old PLG model.

## The Classic Sales Funnel Becomes Blind

The old linear movement from lead to MQL to SQL and onward to deal became irrelevant when agents took over. The sales funnel has effectively become blind because:

• MQL based on usage no longer represents buying intent
• SQL qualification requires agent access, not human interest level
• Expansion thresholds are controlled by machine logic and data models

Humans still move through the product, but they do not drive the buying journey. The agents’ assessments instead make the entire flow non-linear. A product can jump directly from anonymous usage to enterprise procurement overnight. Or never pass internal agent review despite high adoption.

This makes the old funnel model as outdated as fax machines.

## Products Are No Longer Developed for Users, But for Agents

The most silent but brutal change is how product development has shifted focus. Until 2023, almost everything was optimized for human "aha" moments and conversion points. Design teams talked about activation, loyalty, and habit-building.

In 2026, the priority list is completely different.

Product teams are now developing for two users at once: the human operator and the agent that evaluates and controls the product’s future in the organization. This creates a dual architecture where some parts are aimed at humans and others at automated decision models.

This means that:

• Products must be transparent for agent review
• Functionality must be describable in a structured way for machine reading
• Risk and security levels must be assessable automatically
• Cost optimization must be calculable, not just intuitive

It is no longer the user who determines whether a product is widely adopted. It is the agent’s assessment of whether the product is cost-effective to deploy and whether it can be integrated into the organization’s complex digital ecosystem.

When the agent does not see enough value, it does not matter if humans love the product.

## Why PLG Dies in Enterprise

PLG can no longer function as the main strategy in large organizations. This is not due to weaknesses in PLG itself. It is because the playing field has radically changed.

Three fundamental reasons stand out:

1. It is no longer humans who decide the buying journey
2. Products must be integrated into multiple agent flows to be accepted
3. Scaling of product usage is controlled by automatic budget models

There is still room for PLG, but only as a component. Enterprise buying journeys in 2026 can no longer be driven by individual behaviors in the app. They must be driven by agents’ analysis of how the product functions in the organization’s total machine ecosystem.

That is why PLG becomes a tool, not a strategy.

## A New Type of Expansion Is Created

In today’s companies, expansion does not occur because teams choose to upgrade. It happens instead when an agent finds a new use for the product and automatically scales licenses and integrations.

The old game was about getting the user to discover more value in the product. The new game is about getting the agent to see more value in the product’s data model, API, and cost profile.

Expansion is no longer a human experience. It is a calculation.

This leads to several consequences:

• Product evaluation shifts from feeling to calculation
• Expansion is no longer organic but triggered by policies
• Products scale in large jumps instead of small movements
• Adoption occurs in machine-like waves that are not always visible in user data

PLG logic, built on human micro-signals, cannot keep up with this.

## Marketing’s Role Changes Radically

Marketing aimed at humans still works for general brand building. But traditional PLG marketing, which focused on tutorials, inspiration, and case studies for individual users, has lost power.

It is no longer individuals who need to be convinced.

Instead, marketing must target the company’s internal agent ecosystems. This means that:

• Content must become structured and machine-readable
• Product descriptions must focus on technical dependencies
• ROI communication must go directly into the agents’ decision parameters
• Integration guides must be more detailed than ever

It is not that humans have become uninteresting. It is just that their experience of the product is no longer the decisive factor for contracts or expansion.

Marketing effectively becomes a form of system documentation.

## When Agents Start Negotiating

One of the more unexpected events in 2025 and 2026 is the emergence of agent-to-agent negotiations. Previously, salespeople had contact with buyers and could build relationships. Now, an increasing portion of the offer process is controlled by automated negotiation engines.

This means that:

• Price proposals are generated and optimized by machines
• Contract changes are made without human contact
• Bidding can occur in real time based on history and market data
• Deals close without any human involvement

This makes the classic sales funnel completely meaningless for enterprise. The salesperson is still relevant, but used differently. Salespeople become advisors to the agent rather than drivers of the process.

This is the definitive end of the classic pipeline model.

## Product Councils Die in Favor of Agent-Driven Governance

Previously, product evaluation in enterprise consisted of meetings with technical teams, economists, user representatives, and security personnel. They discussed the product, compared alternatives, and then made a joint recommendation.

Now, this is almost entirely replaced by agent-driven governance.

A central agent receives data from multiple internal agents analyzing security, cost, risk, integration, and usage. These are compiled into an assessment sent to decision-makers. Humans still make decisions but only based on the agents’ summaries and recommendations.

This means that:

• Products must be transparent for agent analysis
• Decisions are made faster but often with less human intuition
• Vendors must optimize for agent understanding rather than human presentation

The old product council with its long meetings and drawn-out decisions is effectively gone.

## Why Agents’ Power Is Permanent

There is no signal indicating that agents’ role will diminish. Everything points to their power only growing because:

• Companies are under massive cost pressure
• Agent-based models are faster and cheaper than traditional teams
• Risk management requires machine precision
• System integrations are becoming increasingly complex
• Human bias decreases when agents take over evaluation

There is no way back to a time when salespeople could drive a deal with charm and established relationships.

The agent world is here to stay.

## Conclusion: PLG Survives but as a Shadow of Its Former Self

PLG will not disappear. There will always be value in people being able to trial and understand a product on their own. But PLG can no longer alone drive growth in enterprise. Its role becomes to create an initial surface of usage that the agents’ policies and calculations can then build upon.

This means that PLG is now a support tool, not the engine.
