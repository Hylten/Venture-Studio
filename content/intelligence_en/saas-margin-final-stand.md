SaaS Margins' Final Stand: The Path Back to 90%

## Introduction: Margins Return as a Structural Issue

The modern SaaS industry is experiencing a rare moment of recalibration. For more than a decade, valuations, capital flows, and organizational models were built on the assumption that gross margins would converge toward 80 to 90 percent over time. This figure was not merely an accounting entry but an axiom in the architecture of the digital economy. It provided the space to scale go-to-market engines, expand product breadth, and sustain the entire middle-layer infrastructure required for global distribution.

But over the past five years, margins have eroded. The cost structure has fundamentally shifted. AI-based inference, high-resolution data storage, real-time synchronization, and increasingly complex security frameworks have altered the relationship between value, price, and cost. Gross margins in the 65 to 75 percent range have become normalized, even among Series C+ companies with sophisticated tech stacks. At the same time, the market demands a return to previous levels, forcing companies to rethink everything from architectural principles to distribution models.

The question is not whether SaaS margins can be lifted, but what systemic rebuilds are required to restore the industry to a 90-percent paradigm. This is not about price increases or tactical adjustments, but about a fundamental rewrite of how software is built, run, and delivered.

## Why 90 Percent Matters Again

There are three structural reasons why margins have once again become a defining strategic metric.

First: capital markets’ demand for sustainable profitability has shifted focus from top-line expansion to unit economics. The most capital-efficient companies are those that control their infrastructure costs without compromising the tactical advantages of their product.

Second: the distinction between SaaS and AI-native companies is dissolving. As traditional SaaS platforms integrate generative and predictive functionality, a new cost profile emerges where inference becomes a variable marginal cost rather than a fixed development cost. This creates a need to reinvent gross margin as a design parameter.

Third: market leaders in each category have begun exposing their margin improvement programs, creating competitive pressure. Companies like Notion, Figma, Datadog, and HubSpot have invested in internal AI infrastructure, proprietary optimization layers, and more granular data management. These investments push the boundaries of what is possible and set a pace standard for the entire ecosystem.

Companies that succeed in returning to 90 percent create structural advantages in the form of faster product iteration, more aggressive pricing power, and reduced dependence on exogenous infrastructure providers. They are not just building software, but systems where margin is part of the technical core.

## The Cost Shift No One Predicted

When AI was integrated into SaaS products, it was assumed that margins would temporarily decline before optimizations caught up. Instead, costs have become permanent fixtures. There are three primary drivers behind this cost shift.

The first is inference elasticity. Unlike classic software, costs scale with usage intensity, not the number of users. This means that product shifts from static interactions to generative sessions exponentially increase costs for active customers.

The second is data gravity. As more data is used for fine-tuning, personalization, and contextual analysis, storage and indexing costs rise—especially when data must be kept real-time synchronized and segmented.

The third is the expansion of the security stack. Regulatory complexity, customer requirements, and risk exposure demand more isolation layers, encryption modules, and ML-based anomaly detection, raising per-user costs over time.

The effect is that the traditional cost model is no longer linear. Instead, a multidimensional cost function emerges where compute, token costs, network bandwidth, and data structuring interact in ways that render old margin models obsolete.

## Gross Margin as an Architectural Principle

The modern Series C+ SaaS company is now forced to formulate gross margin as part of technical strategy rather than as a result of it. This means margins must be planned into the stack itself: how data is stored, how AI is used, how interactions are orchestrated, and how each call is transformed and distributed.

Three emerging directions are guiding this recalibration.

First, internal model compilation, model downscaling, and token optimization have become core competencies even for traditional SaaS teams. It is no longer sufficient to integrate external models; companies must understand how computational costs are affected by context length, batching, parallelization, and quantization.

Second, the importance of inference localization is growing. Running generative functions near the customer or in dedicated zones with optimized latency can dramatically reduce costs. Many companies are also building hybrid models where offline inference is combined with real-time to reduce token intensity.

Third, more companies are investing in proprietary data structures. By controlling how data is semantically indexed, costs for retrieval and context building can be reduced. This is especially important in products with high interaction frequency.

From this perspective, the 90-percent margin is not a result of efficiency gains or price hikes, but an emergent property of a well-constructed AI and data architecture.

## The New Margin Drivers: Inference, Data, and Orchestration

To understand the future of SaaS margins, one must consider three infrastructural layers that together determine the cost profile.

The first layer is the inference layer. It encompasses model selection, model compression, quantization, batching, and token composition. This is where the greatest cost volatility arises and where the greatest potential for margin improvement is found.

The second layer is the data layer. As AI-driven products require finer interfaces with customer data, organizations need more granular indexing, faster retrieval, and more sophisticated policies for latency management. Efficient data structures can halve the costs of context building and performance optimization.

The third layer is the orchestration layer. This is often the most overlooked layer, yet it offers the greatest leverage. By controlling when, how, and in what order different modular calls occur, unnecessary generative processes can be reduced, cheaper models can be chosen, and context can be reused where possible.

A mature company takes control of all three layers. Those who optimize only one layer quickly lose the advantage. A coherent strategy is required to build margins that can reach the 85 to 90 percent range.

## Market Leaders’ System Architecture: Three Models

Three architectural patterns have become clear among companies moving toward a new margin standard.

The first model is the proprietary inference loop. Here, custom modules are built for embedding, classification, and composition, reducing dependence on external LLMs except for the heaviest tasks. This makes inference costs more predictable and optimizable internally.

The second model is the retrieval-first architecture. In this structure, generative inference is used only as a final step, after the majority of intelligence has been performed through deterministic retrieval. This reduces context dependence and token costs.

The third model is the hybrid stack. Here, on-device models, specialized inference clusters, and central model hubs are combined to achieve the best possible balance between cost control, latency, and functionality. This pattern takes longer to implement but yields the highest gross margins over time.

In all three cases, it is central that the company owns its own architectural standard. This is the real inflection point for Series C+ companies in the new AI economy.

## The Growing Cost of External Intelligence

Despite major technological shifts, few companies dare to acknowledge how dependent they are on third-party models and cloud infrastructure. This dependency is not only cost-driving but also creates asymmetric risk. Every percentage point increase in pricing from major model providers is multiplied across the entire customer base, creating margin erosion that is difficult to compensate for.

There are three main reasons why the costs of external intelligence continue to rise.

First: model training and inference are capital-intensive processes, forcing providers to optimize their own margins and pass costs on to customers.

Second: competition for compute power creates bottlenecks that make it difficult to maintain cost control over time.

Third: regulatory requirements and AI data certification increase complexity in supply chains, further raising the cost structure.

Companies that do not build their own intelligence infrastructure risk getting stuck in a cost structure that makes 90-percent margins impossible.

## Recentralization of the Stack as a Competitive Tool

In the early SaaS era, decentralization of the stack was a competitive advantage. By outsourcing everything from data storage to recommendation engines, resources could be focused on the core product. But with AI integration, this logic has reversed.

Companies that now take control of their stack gain three advantages.

They achieve predictable margins because they control a larger portion of the cost structure.

They reduce latency in the product’s intelligence layer, increasing user value.

They become less sensitive to price changes and capacity shortages from external providers.

Recentralization is therefore not just a technical strategy, but an economic necessity. It is a choice that determines which companies survive in a world where intelligence is as central as the software itself.

## Scalability Loops and Margin Elasticity

In traditional SaaS, margin expansion occurred through a simple mechanism: each new customer was added to a shared infrastructure without cost increasing proportionally. But AI introduces a new type of elasticity. Cost no longer scales linearly with the user base, but with interaction depth.

This means companies must construct scalability loops that explicitly account for margin elasticity. This could involve:

Product architecture that reduces inference frequency.

Model pipelines that scale based on task complexity.

Data indexes that reduce the need for contextual generation.

These mechanisms are central to returning to a margin profile that makes the SaaS model attractive again. But the work is technically advanced and requires competencies that few organizations have in-house.

## The Path Forward: The Three Remaining Barriers to Overcome

Companies that win the final stand of SaaS margins will need to solve three fundamental barriers.

The first barrier is the energy cost of inference. Even optimized LLM and multimodal models require significant energy. Innovation is needed in both software and data center architecture.

The second barrier is data coherence. As datasets become increasingly fragmented, the risk of inefficient retrieval increases, driving up both costs and latency. Companies need to develop data cores optimized for AI.

The third barrier is model fragmentation. The combination of specialized models, retrieval models, and generalist models makes the architecture difficult to control. Fragmentation must be replaced by modular, coordinated systems.

Companies that address these barriers will become the next generation’s category leaders. But the work is complex and requires organizations to develop a new type of core competence: system building.

## The Renaissance of System Building

System building is about constructing wholes where each layer interacts with the others in a way that creates emergent advantages. It is the opposite of point solutions. In an AI-driven SaaS architecture, system building means that data flows, inference pipelines, and product logic must be unified into a single coherent system.

A new type of architecture is emerging. It is characterized by:

Intelligence being as close to the user as possible.

The data structure being optimized for retrieval, not storage.

Inference being distributed across multiple optimized compute layers.

The cost model being embedded in the architecture.

It is in this system building that margins are found. The path to 90 percent does not go through cost savings, but through technical reconstruction of the SaaS model.

And this reconstruction has only just begun. Most companies are still at the threshold of the new era, trying to understand how their stack needs to be reshaped to be competitive in a world where AI is not a layer, but the core of the product.

The real question is therefore not whether SaaS margins will return, but which companies understand what actually needs to be built to get there. It is a question of architecture, of data, of inference, but above all, of which organizations dare...

## The New Cost Structure That Grates

When reviewing the development of the SaaS industry over the past decade, a pattern emerges that is both logical and alarming. The logical part is that complexity tends to drive costs. The alarming part is that much of the cost mass built up is not directly linked to customer value.

During the early expansion phase, expectations were different. The market rewarded rapid growth above all else, and companies could justify increasing operating costs because revenue multiples were almost unlimited. But alongside this, another development occurred. Functionality increased, customer demands grew, and product teams expanded at a rate few questioned. Eventually, many companies found themselves with product organizations sized for three years of future ambition, not for today’s actual needs.

When interest rates rose and capital markets shifted, the calculation changed immediately. The old truth that software scales almost for free proved only to hold if both product design and internal processes were also built for scale. It turned out that many were not.

So the question now is: how do you move from today’s margin situation back to 90 percent without losing momentum in product development and without degrading the customer experience?

## Why 90 Percent Is Not a Nostalgic Level

There is a tendency to talk about historical SaaS margins as something mythical that only worked during a unique market period. But that picture is wrong. 90 percent is not a nostalgic benchmark but a practical consequence of what software actually is. Once a function is built, it has no physical cost. It does not need to be produced again. It does not need to be physically distributed. It does not wear out.

This makes software one of the most scalable business formats available. To reach margins close to 90 percent, therefore, does not require magic. It only requires that the following four conditions are in place.

The product architecture must be modular and easy to further develop.
The tech stack must be cost-effective and modern.
Customer value must be clear and not require heavy manual support.
The internal organization must minimize duplication and parallel initiatives.

Companies that today still maintain margins in the 80–90 percent range almost always exhibit precisely these characteristics.

## The Silent Margin Drain

There are three main categories of margin-draining problems that have built up in the SaaS sector, often without being caught until it is too late.

First, there are technical debt issues that make new functionality take longer than it should because old code must be supported. This leads to larger teams and higher payroll costs.

Second, we have customer-facing costs that have increased as customers have become more advanced in their requirements. Implementation, customer support, and customer success teams have grown at almost the same rate as sales in many companies. This is directly visible in gross margins because most of this cost falls above the line.

Finally, there are integration costs. Companies now build for a world of interconnected systems where API standards, third-party platforms, and data requirements all contribute to elevated technical costs. Creating and maintaining integrations is expensive, and the result is clearly visible in margin levels.

It is this combination of technical debt, customer-facing costs, and integration dependencies that has slowly eaten away at SaaS’s most valuable asset: scalability.

## Changing Customer Behavior Poses New Demands

Another development rarely mentioned in margin debates is that buying behavior has changed. Customers today are less willing to accept generic solutions. They want customization, advice, and a partner that solves their specific problems. This can create success in sales but also increases the cost per customer.

Ten years ago, a SaaS product could be launched with a limited onboarding process. The customer logged in, clicked around, and any questions were resolved via FAQ. Today, even mid-sized customers expect everything from workshops to customized dashboards. Delivering this often requires consulting hours rather than pure software.

Companies that manage to keep margins intact often do so by actively steering customer behavior. They provide advanced functionality to the largest customers at premium fees. They standardize processes and set clear limits on what is included. They train customers to use the product instead of relying on services.

The bottom line is that high margins are not just a technical issue. It is also a behavioral economics question. How much extra work are customers willing to pay for, and how much is the company willing to include in the price?

## The Recurring Mistrust of Technical Investments

During downturns, technology organizations often receive mostly reactive budgets. Management wants to see cost reductions rather than investments, and this can lock companies into old architectures that in turn drive up costs further. When investments in scalability are deferred year after year, the problem becomes exponential.

It is not possible to return to 90 percent if you cling to technical debt for nostalgic or budgetary reasons. What is required is the courage to pause initiatives that do not build the core product and replace them with initiatives that reduce technical friction.

Technical investments that reduce costs are rarely glamorous. They are not visible externally and do not generate press releases. But they determine margin levels over time.

## Where AI Fits into the Margin Discussion

AI has become something of a universal answer to cost problems, but the reality is more nuanced. AI can both improve and worsen margins depending on how it is implemented.

AI can increase margins by:

Automating support and thereby reducing cost per customer.
Simplifying documentation and product development.
Reducing the need for manual QA.
Optimizing operating costs through smarter resource allocation.

At the same time, AI can worsen margins if:

Model runs become too expensive due to incorrect architecture.
The company builds AI features customers are unwilling to pay extra for.
Integration costs with external AI platforms exceed projections.
The organization grows instead of shrinking despite automation.

The crucial factor is therefore not whether a company uses AI, but how. From a margin perspective, the best AI investment is often the one that is invisible to the end customer but reduces internal friction.

## The Difficult Balance Between High Margins and High Growth

There is a misconception that high margins and high growth are two opposing poles. In reality, it is often a sign of health when a company can combine both. The challenge lies in the fact that many companies have used growth as an excuse for inefficiency, and when margin requirements are then raised, the adjustment becomes dramatic.

A typical journey over the past three years has looked like this:

Expansion into more customer segments without changing the product’s core logic.
Increased complexity in both product and organization.
Declining margins.
Market shift and demand for profitability.
Cost savings that do not address the root of the problem.
Stagnation in product development.

Truly strong SaaS companies break this cycle by keeping the product lean, the organization efficient, and the customer offering clear. Margins then become a consequence of a deliberate active choice, not a result of luck or market conditions.

## The Path Forward Requires a New Type of Leadership

Returning to 90 percent is not about cutting costs. It is about shaping a product and an organization that are dimensioned for how software works today rather than how it worked in single-digit interest rate environments.

The type of leadership required has three clear characteristics.

Deep understanding of technical scalability. Leaders must be able to see the connections between code choices, architectural decisions, and margins.

Communicative clarity about what is included in the product and what is a service. This is fundamental to avoiding margin erosion.

Courage to prioritize long-term investments over short-term cosmetic results.

The SaaS leader who meets these three requirements can return to 90 percent, regardless of market fluctuations.

## Summary of the Final Stand of Margins

The SaaS industry is not in free fall. It is undergoing an adjustment that should have happened earlier. The past decade created a cost structure that does not harmonize with software’s fundamental nature. Returning to high margins does not require magic or brutal cuts. It requires a return to simplicity.

Companies that succeed will be those that:

Build scalable architecture.
Standardize customer processes.
Reduce technical debt.
Use AI for cost optimization, not as a marketing tool.
Steer customer behavior rather than follow it.

This is the final stand of SaaS margins. A return to the original idea of software. A product that costs almost nothing to deliver once it is built.
