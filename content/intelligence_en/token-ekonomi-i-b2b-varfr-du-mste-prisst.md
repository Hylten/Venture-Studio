Token Economics in B2B: Why You Must Price by Energy, Not Users

## Introduction: When B2B Models Meet Energy-Based Systems

For the past twenty years, B2B SaaS has been shaped by a relatively stable economic logic: user-based pricing, modular add-ons, and gradual expansion via seats. This model was rational in a world where marginal cost for additional usage was near zero and where software primarily revolved around data storage, permissions, and interface management.

But when applications become autonomously driven by models, inference layers, and data processes, a new economic reality emerges. AI systems consume energy in the form of tokens, compute, latency budgets, and data transformations. These energy flows are not linear with the number of users and can sometimes scale faster than the customer’s organization realizes. The result is that user-based pricing becomes dysfunctional in a world where value creation and cost structure are increasingly governed by automated, energy-intensive pipelines.

This text serves as a systems study of why B2B SaaS must accept a token-based future. It is not about repackaging GPT calls as credits. It is about building a pricing model that reflects the underlying energy dynamics of a system, where each component has its own operational cost, scaling curve, and risk profile.

Token economics becomes a necessity because it ties pricing to the system’s real friction points rather than to human logins. It is a prerequisite for building sustainable, high-margin Series C+ companies in an AI infrastructure-driven market.

## Why User-Based Pricing Collapses in AI-First SaaS

When companies build applications around LLM models, embeddings, automated decision logic, and pipeline-based processing, fundamental economic relationships change. The traditional link between a user and production cost weakens and is replaced by a link between function and energy consumption.

Three structural drivers underpin the shift:

1. **Automation drives consumption even when users are inactive**
2. **Costs arise within model inference flows, not in the UI layer**
3. **Scaling occurs through agent systems and compute orchestration, not through more seats**

When a model automatically generates summaries every hour, when autonomous workflows run across an entire database, when continuous embeddings are updated, or when an agent decides to expand its search scope, energy consumption arises that no user explicitly caused. Under a user-based pricing model, the provider risks subsidizing large amounts of compute not tied to any billable unit.

In many AI-driven systems, the opposite happens. The most cost-driving processes are those that eliminate the need for more users. This means traditional metric expansion is no longer tied to the cost base.

When seats no longer represent value or load, they become an artificial friction in sales and a barrier to enterprise adoption. This is especially clear in organizations with interoperable workflows where AI acts across team boundaries and where value is created by the system rather than by individuals.

## Energy as First Principle: Tokens, Compute, and Friction Points

In a token economy, the system is viewed as a circuit of energy. Token consumption is not just a measure of language model length. It represents a proxy for the entire chain of system consumption: retrieval of knowledge, transformation of data, execution of model steps, and generation of new application state.

Energy in AI systems is distributed across three levels:

**Level 1: Inference Energy**
Direct model tokens in and out. This is the most intuitive level and the one most SaaS companies spontaneously try to price around. But value flows do not arise solely here.

**Level 2: Pipeline Energy**
All work around the model: RAG layers, embeddings, indexing, data movement, API chains, re-ranking, tool calls, data cleaning, and normalization. This level can consume more energy than inference itself, especially with enterprise-scale datasets.

**Level 3: System Energy**
Autonomously emergent work: decision-making, agent dynamics, parallelization, self-initiated iteration, error handling, and backoff logic. This is where complex AI systems resemble distributed microservices with probabilistic behavior profiles.

When companies price based on a seat model, they try to bind these three levels into a single metric indicator. This is technically unreasonable. Energy flows do not increase with the number of people but with the system’s internal behavior. The result is that user prices become either artificially high or dangerously low.

## Why Token Economics Is Not the Same as Usage Billing

There is a widespread misconception that usage billing is the same as token economics. It is not. Usage billing traditionally measures frequency, number of calls, or amount of data. Token economics is a structural model where energy flows function as the primary unit for value measurement, risk management, and cost prediction.

Token economics involves three fundamental shifts:

1. **Pricing is tied to the system’s internal energy usage, not to superficial API calls**
2. **The economy is built to handle the volatility of autonomous processes**
3. **Incentives are adjusted so the customer can optimize their own data structure for lower energy consumption**

Usage billing measures what the customer does. Token economics measures what the system must do to create the state the customer wants.

This difference is central because it describes a future where AI applications act as energy consumers in an autonomous compute park, rather than as tools for manual users. Companies building pricing models based on usage billing will face a growing mismatch between cost base and revenue model as their systems become more autonomous.

## The System Builder’s Perspective: Why Energy Becomes the Only Stable Pricing Model

When building AI systems at the enterprise level, one question becomes central: what unit can we use to price functionality in a stable, scalable, and future-proof way?

There are five requirements for such a unit:

1. It must correlate with marginal cost
2. It must correlate with the customer’s perceived value
3. It must be traceable, auditable, and predictable
4. It must not hinder adoption
5. It must be agnostic to future technological shifts

Seats meet only requirement 4.

Modular pricing meets requirement 2 partially.

API calls meet requirements 3 and sometimes 1.

Token or energy-based pricing meets all five.

The reason is that energy is the most elemental resource in AI systems. It is also the only resource that will always be consumed in proportion to the value generated, because every new outcome requires some form of transformation.

It does not matter if future models are local, distributed, multimodal, continuous, or generative. They will always consume energy. Therefore, energy is the only pricing model that is technically future-proof.

## The Customer’s Perspective: Optimization, Not Limitation

Companies will not see token economics as a cost but as an optimization opportunity.

In a seat-based model, there is no optimization function. The customer can do nothing more than buy more or fewer seats. In a token economy, the customer can optimize:

- **Data quality:** Datasets with low redundancy and more structured context consume fewer tokens.
- **Workflows:** A pipeline system clearly orchestrated consumes less energy than one built on duplicated queries and recurring searches.
- **Permission structure:** Limited access can reduce the need for broad, expensive queries.
- **Prompt structure:** Standardized, consistent prompts can halve an application’s energy needs.
- **Integration depth:** When data resides close to the inference layer, transfer costs decrease.

Token economics turns the customer into an actor in the system’s efficiency, not a passive buyer of seats. This leads to a more sustainable relationship between provider and customer, where both parties are invested in the system’s stability.

## Token Economics Solves Enterprise Frictions Seats Create

Enterprise customers tend to be skeptical of user-based models because they create organizational and legal frictions. Seats mean:

- Endless internal coordination: Every team must approve how many users get access. This creates delays.
- IT governance blocks adoption: Onboarding new users often requires internal approvals, data protection reviews, and contract changes.
- Budgeting becomes difficult: When price depends on the number of people rather than system outcomes, ROI is hard to assess.
- Automation becomes a threat: If automation reduces the number of users, enterprise customers can end up in a paradox where they pay more to get less usage.

Token economics instead allows enterprise customers to budget based on system function rather than internal organizational structure. It unlocks faster adoption, fewer purchasing barriers, and clearer accountability.

## AI Agents Make Seat Models Technically Impossible

When AI agents become primary users of systems, seat models become technically absurd. Who should be billed for an agent? How many seats correspond to five conversing agent groups with asynchronous communication? Is an agent a user or an internal process? How is an agent that initiates an agent tree decision counted?

Token economics solves this by not trying to model agents as humans. They are seen as energy consumers. An agent tree with five levels costs more energy than a simple instruction. This follows the system’s natural cost structure and is directly auditable.

This is why token economics is not just a pricing model but also a foundation for how AI systems should be understood, dimensioned, and controlled in organizations building advanced, autonomous workflows.

## The Coming Enterprise Standard: Energy as Contract Level

Enterprise will require three things in all AI contracts:

- **Energy budget:** How much energy is allocated per month, quarter, or year.
- **Energy profiles:** Which system components consume the most and why.
- **Energy operational policy:** Rules for what happens when the energy budget is reached, what fallback modes are required, and how limiting is handled.

This creates an ecosystem where token economics is not just a billing model but part of the governance framework. Enterprise cannot be expected to buy AI systems that behave like black boxes with unknown consumption. They will want transparency at the energy level, just as they require SLAs, audit logs, and data lineage.

Token economics enables this. Seats do not.

## The Real Point: Token Economics Reflects the System’s Physics

All modern technological development eventually falls back on a simple principle: pricing must reflect underlying physics. For data centers, it is energy and cooling. For networks, it is bandwidth and latency. For storage, it is volume and redundancy. For AI, it is tokens, compute, and pipeline work.

B2B companies that do not adapt their pricing models to these physical relationships will face problems the moment their products become autonomous enough that their previous economic model no longer reflects reality.

Token economics is not a trend. It is a consequence of AI systems’ nature. And all consequences eventually become standards.

## Why Energy-Based Pricing Is the Only Realistic Model for AI-First B2B

It is easy to believe token economics is a technical side issue only engineers need to worry about. But in reality, it is a fundamental shift in how value is created, costs arise, and business models function. Where traditional SaaS relied on users, modules, and feature bundles, AI-driven products must be understood through the energy required to generate each output. In a world where AI is the engine, what is measured and monetized must be the fuel itself.

When companies believe they can continue with per-seat or per-user models, they fall into a strategic trap: they price something that is no longer a scarce resource. Users are no longer the variable driving cost or value. Energy is. Energy consists of token input, token output, and the underlying compute cost that does the work.

It is in this shift that many B2B players risk falling behind. Those who do not understand the energy economics of AI will be stuck in margin pressure, unpredictable costs, and a product architecture that cannot scale.

## The Real Problem: Selling Something the Customer No Longer Needs to Pay For

When a tool integrates AI, the user becomes less active. Processes previously requiring human intervention become automated or semi-automated. This means traditional user-based revenue logic loses its foundation. A customer does not need more user accounts to achieve more—they need more compute cycles to perform more work.

This creates a paradox for companies clinging to an old model: the customer’s business can grow without the supplier’s revenue growing. In the past, this was impossible. More users meant more licenses. Now, an entire department can perform its work through a single AI agent. When this happens without the price following, the company’s gross margin erodes slowly and invisibly. Eventually, the cost per customer exceeds what the customer pays, even if revenue initially looked stable.

Energy-based pricing solves this by tying revenue to real value. The customer pays for work done, not for human presence in a system.

## How Energy Economics Changes Customer Relationships

Many believe energy-based pricing would make customer relationships more transactional. In practice, the opposite happens. When customers see exactly how much energy they consume, how much value is produced, and how costs are distributed across workflows, a new type of transparency emerges. Customers can make better decisions. Providers can explain value more clearly.

This makes it possible to:

- Track cost drivers in real time
- Optimize processes based on energy consumption
- Identify inefficient usage
- Predict monthly consumption without guesswork

This shifts the customer relationship from license administration to partnership around productivity, efficiency, and workflows. Instead of questions like "How many users can we remove?" the dialogue becomes "How can we reduce our energy consumption for report generation by 20%?"

This is a far more business-relevant conversation.

## Internal Shift: Product Organizations Must See Themselves as Compute Capital Operators

In the SaaS era, it was enough to build a product users liked and price it in a way that allowed the company to grow. Now, product organizations must understand their cost structure at a level previously only infrastructure companies needed to.

Every feature, every flow, every user journey consumes energy. This means a product manager must:

- Know the cost per operation
- Optimize model selection and configurations
- Balance latency against cost
- Choose the right context lengths
- Design prompts within cost budgets

There is no way around this. If the product is not optimized for energy consumption, it will lose competitiveness either through excessively high internal costs or through prices the market will not accept. This shift is as significant as when the cloud made local servers obsolete. The difference is that the change is happening faster.

## The Customer Will Compare You to the Cost of Doing It Themselves

When customers understand energy economics, they quickly realize they can calculate exactly what it would cost to build their own solution using, for example, OpenAI, Anthropic, or Google. This was not possible in the old SaaS model. Value was fuzzy, and the customer’s alternatives were often unclear.

Now, a customer can calculate:

- Cost per 1 million tokens
- Cost for fine-tuning
- Cost for inference at different context lengths
- Cost for vector-based search
- Cost for hosting their own model

This means your price must be reasonable relative to the actual energy you use. Margins can still be created through better product design, better models, better infrastructure, and better workflows. But the customer’s ability to compare is significantly greater than before.

Transparency is not just an advantage—it is a requirement.

## Why Fixed Pricing Is Dangerous in AI Products

It may seem reassuring to offer fixed pricing, especially if customers are unfamiliar with energy-based models. But fixed pricing means you take on the customer’s consumption risk. If your estimate is wrong, costs can explode without revenue following.

There are three typical scenarios where fixed pricing becomes dangerous:

1. The customer starts using a feature automatically at a much larger scale than you anticipated
2. Model costs change upward with little notice
3. The customer’s business volume grows but your price is locked

With AI, two of these often happen simultaneously. For example, if the customer integrates your AI product into their ERP or CRM, usage can multiply overnight. If you are stuck with fixed pricing, every new request becomes a margin loss.

Energy is the only variable that always moves in line with the customer’s actual usage.

## The Customer’s Psychological Image of Value Changes

Previously, the customer paid for a tool and then tried to extract as much value as possible from it. Now, the customer pays for value directly. This changes how they experience the purchasing process. Many customers feel, for the first time, that they are treated fairly. They pay only when they produce something—not for having an account, not for having a team, not for someone to click a button.

There is a psychological dimension where energy-based pricing creates:

- A sense of control
- A sense of scalability
- A sense that the product is working, not the customer

This is the same feeling the cloud gave when moving from physical servers to pay-as-you-go. It was not just economically attractive—it was a mental relief.

## Token Economics Makes the Product’s Value Measurable in a New Way

When energy becomes the fundamental pricing unit, value production can be analyzed at a completely different level. It becomes possible to calculate:

- Cost per generated report
- Cost per qualified lead
- Cost per automated support ticket
- Cost per completed analysis
- Cost per marketing content

This makes ROI measurable down to the smallest unit. For the first time, AI work can be systematically compared to human work. It becomes possible to say:

- This process previously cost 200 SEK in human time and five minutes.
- With AI, it costs 40 öre in energy and two seconds.

This is a completely new type of business dialogue that was not possible in software before, because traditional tools did not do the work for the customer—they just helped a human do it.

## Risks for B2B Companies That Do Not Switch to Energy-Based Pricing

There are three risks that recur in companies that do not understand token economics in time.

**First risk:** Costs spiral when AI features are used in production. When customers start integrating features into their workflows, consumption increases exponentially. Without an energy-based model, this goes straight into the company’s costs and eats margins.

**Second risk:** Under-revenue. When value production increases but revenue stays flat, the company loses room for innovation, staff, and technical development. This happens silently and is often not visible until it is too late.

**Third risk:** Becoming irrelevant compared to competitors offering transparent and energy-optimized pricing. Customers will start preferring solutions where they can predict costs exactly.

When a market has adapted to energy economics, it is almost impossible to return to per-seat models.

## Why This Shift Is Irreversible

There is no future where user-based pricing makes a comeback in AI-intensive B2B products. All logic points in the same direction:

- Costs follow energy
- Value follows energy
- Scalability follows energy
- Customer comparison benchmarks follow energy
- Automation reduces the need for user accounts

Technically, economically, and psychologically, energy-based pricing is the only defensible model long-term. The earlier a company adapts, the greater the chance it can build a sustainable business model in a market where AI is no longer an option but the infrastructure for all work.
