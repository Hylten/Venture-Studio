## The New Consolidation Order

The vertical software market entered 2026 with a consolidation structure that did not exist in earlier cycles. Private equity funds, strategic acquirers, and venture studios now bid on the same fragmented assets. Studios win the small-cap segment because their cost of capital and integration machinery match the asset class.

Fragmented vertical software shares a profile: 5 to 50 MSEK in revenue, one dominant workflow, a founder who built the product alone, and a customer base that never demanded more. These companies trade at 4 to 8x EBITDA when they trade at all. Strategic buyers skip the segment because the revenue is too small to move a public number.

The arbitrage sits in the spread between that segment and the broader software market. Private SaaS companies closed 2025 at a median 22.4x EBITDA, with top performers above 46x. A roll-up that buys at 6x, integrates, and sells at 12x captures the difference in every asset it touches.

## Why the Roll-Up Logic Changed

Traditional roll-ups created value through consolidation and cost cutting. The play combined fragmented businesses, stripped duplicate overhead, and scaled procurement. That logic worked while the underlying operations were stable and the synergies were administrative.

AI roll-ups create value through automation and shared data instead. The consolidator distributes AI models, data, and workflows across every acquired company, and margins expand faster than any headcount reduction allows. Deal teams that closed 180 technology and SaaS transactions describe the shift as moving from efficiency to automation, with the buyer universe now including PE platforms, venture-backed holding companies, and product-led technology firms.

Three market conditions forced the change. Capital is tighter, so founders and investors build leaner businesses.

AI sits in production workflows with measurable output. A new buyer class actively prices automation into bids.

The service layer matters as much as the product. Vertical tools sit inside workflows that still run on labor, and the roll-up that automates the surrounding service layer captures margin the product alone never touched. That is why AI roll-ups concentrate in service-heavy verticals first.

## The Multiple Landscape

The valuation floor for vertical software reset after the 2022 peak. EBITDA multiples ran 15 to 20x from 2015 to 2019, hit 39.9x in the second half of 2022, and settled into a 17 to 22x range through 2023 and 2024.

The second half of 2025 brought a normalization toward 26.1x as operating scale improved across the market. The spread between segments is where the roll-up lives. Data infrastructure companies trade at 24.4x and DevOps platforms at 36.5x, while small vertical tools sit at 4 to 8x.

For a studio, the spread is not a market statistic, it is a portfolio instruction. Every acquired tool that moves from the 4 to 8x band into the 17 to 22x range transfers the delta to the studio balance sheet. Portfolio construction becomes the arbitrage.

That price gap explains the buyer migration. PE firms, venture platforms, and product-led companies all pursue acquisitions where AI meaningfully shifts the economics. The asset class now has a dedicated investor sentiment survey, a growing buyer universe, and a valuation model still in flux.

## Capital Cost Dictates the Buyer

The buyer that wins an asset class carries the lowest cost of capital for that risk profile. PE funds raise equity against fund-level return hurdles of 20 to 25 percent IRR, which forces them toward larger platforms where the equity check justifies the diligence cost. Small vertical tools fail that test before the first meeting.

A 20 MSEK revenue tool with 6 MSEK EBITDA is too small for a 5 billion SEK fund. The same asset is a building block for a studio that owns three adjacent tools and shares one engineering team. The studio converts the asset into portfolio capacity instead of fund carry.

Studios finance acquisitions from the studio balance sheet, operating cash flow, and small debt facilities. The capital base carries no external IRR mandate, so studios hold assets that a fund must exit inside a defined window. Holding power changes the underwriting, and the underwriting changes the price.

The mandate gap produces the bid. A fund needs the roll-up to double within four years to satisfy its return hurdle. A studio needs the combined entity to generate positive operating scale, which is a lower bar and a higher price.

## The Studio Structural Advantage

Studios run the infrastructure the roll-up requires before the first acquisition. Shared engineering, shared data pipelines, shared go-to-market, and one product core serve every portfolio company. An acquired company plugs into that core instead of rebuilding it.

Integration cost drops from a multi-year project to a quarter of model deployment, data migration, and customer communication. The studio writes the integration playbook once and replays it across the portfolio, which converts a fixed cost into a marginal cost. Private equity buyers write the playbook fresh on every platform, and the difference shows in the post-close burn.

Talent follows the same logic. The scarce resource in vertical AI is not capital, it is engineers who understand both the vertical workflow and the model layer. Studios retain those engineers because the portfolio gives them more surface area than any standalone 20-person company.

Product-led technology companies enter the same market with the same AI thesis. They lack the shared infrastructure, so their integration cost lands higher and their synergy math depends on headcount cuts that destroy founder-led culture. The customers who trust a founder-built tool notice the difference and churn.

Deal sourcing follows the same structural logic. Studios see the market through their portfolio: adjacent tools surface in customer calls, partner conversations, and competitor analysis before they reach a broker. The pipeline is proprietary by construction, and proprietary pipelines price better than auction processes.

## The Integration Economy: LTV/CAC in SEK

The unit economics of a vertical roll-up work in SEK, and they work because the model layer compounds across acquisitions. Each new asset feeds the shared stack, and the stack lifts every asset that follows.

Example: A studio acquires a Swedish vertical tool with 1,000 customers, 30,000 SEK average revenue per customer, and 90 percent gross margin. Revenue lands at 30 MSEK, LTV at 270,000 SEK per customer with 10 percent annual churn, and CAC at 22,500 SEK. LTV/CAC sits at 12.0, far above the 3.0 threshold that justifies paid acquisition.

The model layer changes the second-order math. When the studio deploys its shared AI stack into the acquired tool, churn drops because the product starts doing work customers previously did manually. A churn reduction from 10 to 7 percent lifts LTV from 270,000 to 385,000 SEK at constant pricing, and LTV/CAC moves from 12.0 to 17.1.

EBITDA follows the same curve. A tool with 30 MSEK revenue at 25 percent EBITDA margin produces 7.5 MSEK EBITDA, and at 6x the asset prices at 45 MSEK.

At 9x, the band buyers pay for AI-integrated vertical software, the same asset prices at 67.5 MSEK. The 22.5 MSEK gap is the automation premium, and it accrues to the consolidator that owns the model layer.

The compounding accelerates at portfolio scale. Five integrated tools share one model layer, one data schema, and one renewal motion, and each marginal acquisition adds less cost than the previous one. The fifth acquisition carries an integration cost below 20 percent of the first, which is the scale advantage a fund cannot replicate without owning the infrastructure.

## Edge Cases That Break the Model

The roll-up math fails in defined conditions, and buyers should map them before the first acquisition.

Founder dependency breaks the retention model. A vertical tool where the founder personally handles 60 percent of support tickets and every renewal conversation loses its retention value after the exit. The churn assumptions above describe product-driven retention, not founder-driven retention.

Data fragmentation kills the automation premium. Vertical tools hold value in workflow data, and when customers store the critical records in spreadsheets while the product only handles the edges, the shared AI stack has nothing to train on. The automation premium never materializes.

Pricing ceilings bind the model. Small vertical tools serve customers who benchmark price against manual labor, and once the automated product exceeds the customer's manual cost, churn spikes regardless of the LTV math. The premium disappears exactly when the product works best.

Integration debt consumes the spread. Studios that acquire five tools with five data schemas inherit a migration problem that eats the entire automation premium, so the consolidation order matters: compatible data models first. Sellers who document their schemas and APIs protect the buyer's premium and their own price.

Seller expectations produce deal collapse. Founders anchor to the 22.4x median even when their asset trades in the 4 to 8x segment, and the expectation gap kills processes. Studios need a disciplined valuation process that documents the automation premium before the letter of intent.

## Summary

The vertical AI roll-up is the consolidation event of this cycle, and venture studios hold the structural advantage: the lowest cost of capital for the asset class, shared integration infrastructure, and a model layer that compounds across every acquisition. The unit economics work in SEK when churn responds to automation and the data model survives migration. Buyers who skip the edge case analysis acquire the arbitrage and watch it dissolve in integration debt and founder dependency.
